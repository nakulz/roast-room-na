# Frameworks: Shreyas Doshi

24 frameworks, most-used first, built from the verified notes in batches 01 to 03 (S001 to S055, no S004) and the source table. Names are his where he named them. A short name in parentheses is unnamed in the sources. How-to-score lines are guidance from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. Product strategy: where to play and how to win
- **What it is:** The prior for focus. A real strategy is a rigorous account of where to play and how to win, over about a 3 to 5 year outlook, that a team can remember and turn into action. A vision, a mission, OKRs, a roadmap, a project plan, unbridled optimism, building an MVP and then iterating, or a wish to be the best is not a strategy.
- **Parts:** He names four failure modes (unnamed): acceptance (strategy does not matter), creation (there is none), substance (it is flawed), and communication (the team cannot recall it) (S019). He also describes strategy as seeing reality, simulating futures, creative choice-making, and tying choices to actions (S027). Components he repeats: situation, target and non-target segments, differentiation, distribution, a roadmap, and a short action plan (S012, S028, S035). A doc is 3 to 7 pages, written by a team of three or fewer (a market expert, a creative product expert, a tech expert), not by a committee (S022, S048). Foundation he points to, and does not claim as his own: Porter's forces and generic strategies, Helmer's 7 Powers, and Rumelt (S022, S035, S048).
- **When to use:** Before prioritization, before annual planning, and whenever the team is busy relitigating the same choices. With a real strategy already aligned, his Stripe Connect plan took about three days instead of four to six weeks (S002).
- **How to score:** Fail if the opposite of the hypothesis would not be a viable strategy for a different company (S048), if it tries to be all things to all people, or if it is a laundry list (S035). Pass if it names who is in and who is out, shows the trade-off, maps choices to actions, and prioritization talk actually refers to it (S027, S035). He also wants every teammate able to state it on a short elevator ride (S015). Numbers in a plan can be rough. False precision on headcount is a waste (S002, S043).
- **Quote:** "if you have a real product strategy, a real one that everybody is aligned with, that you have got pre-alignment on, then a lot of this nonsense we tend to do with annual planning actually goes away." (S002)
- **Sources:** 13 (S002, S009, S012, S015, S016, S019, S022, S027, S028, S035, S043, S047, S048)

## 2. High agency
- **What it is:** Finding a way without waiting for perfect conditions and without blaming the circumstances. He says Eric Weinstein coined the term, and that it matched how he had already been describing the people who outran their credentials (S001). It is a hiring filter and a personal standard, with a cost he names later.
- **Parts:** In the PM version he names strong ownership, creative execution, and resilience (S001). In the fuller thread, ownership mindset is the most important trait, creative execution is the skill, and he relates it to jugaad and to Covey's circle of influence (S025). His matrix: game changers are talented and high agency, go-getters are high agency, frustrated geniuses are talented and low agency. Integrity is a third factor he will not trade. He also lists high agency as a required PM trait or ability (S012, S033, S037, S038) and as one of five high-ROI concepts (S020).
- **When to use:** Hiring, staffing a hard bet, and diagnosing a talented person whose impact is stuck. Also when a team is waiting on permissions, headcount, or a cleaner setup before it will focus.
- **How to score:** Prefer a go-getter over a frustrated genius. A game changer is worth recruiting, and integrity still wins if the two conflict (S025). Low agency sounds like every problem is someone else's. High agency can be learned, and it is not easy. Limit, from 2022: a very high-agency person internalizes failures that were not only theirs, and over time that is emotionally taxing (S054).
- **Quote:** "High Agency is about finding a way to get what you want, without waiting for conditions to be perfect or otherwise blaming the circumstances." (S025)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S008, S012, S020, S023, S025, S033, S037, S038, S054)

## 3. LNO (Leverage, Neutral, Overhead)
- **What it is:** Sort tasks by return, then spend effort to match. He did not invent the underlying idea that tasks are unequal. An HBR article on task prioritization by Elizabeth Grace Saunders spurred it during his first three years at Google, and over about a year he adapted that principle into LNO for product work (S003). Very few items are L. Product strategy and the roadmap are his examples.
- **Parts:** L tasks return 10x or 100x. N tasks, in the 2022 conversation and in the November 2024 essay, are about 1.0 of effort for about 1.1x back. O tasks return less than you put in. A 2024 short post rounds the same scale to L above 10x, N about 1x, and O below 1x (S045). The newer full statement is the 19 November 2024 essay, which keeps 1.1x for neutral (S003). Labels are situational: the same bug, note, or resourcing sheet can move between L, N, and O depending on who reads it and what is at stake (S001, S003). Tactics: prefix the list L, N, or O; do L work at peak energy and often in a new place; front-load N and O a day or two ahead so the hard task starts from a feeling of progress (he calls that placebo productivity) (S001, S003, S010).
- **When to use:** A high-leverage person's list, a meeting load, or a perfectionist who is treating email like strategy. It is a personal operating system. By 2024 he also says scope can overwhelm any efficiency tool, including this one (S002).
- **How to score:** Bulk of hours and all of the perfectionism go to L. N gets a solid job and nothing heroic. O gets a deliberately bad job, because perfectionism will still leave it serviceable (S003, S021). There is no fixed rubric (S003). He says it is not the perfect system for everyone (S003, S010).
- **Quote:** "Leverage tasks: where the effort has a 10x (or even 100x) return." (S003)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S003, S010, S012, S021, S036, S037, S045)

## 4. Pre-mortem with Tigers, Paper Tigers, and Elephants
- **What it is:** A meeting that assumes the project has already failed, so the team can say the causes while there is still time. He does not claim the pre-mortem. He cites Gary Klein's HBR article for the base (the project failed, everyone writes why, each person reads one reason, the facilitator looks for ways to strengthen the plan) and says the standard version did not stick after the meeting (S005). His addition is the lexicon: Tiger, Paper Tiger, Elephant.
- **Parts:** A tiger is a real threat that will hurt you if you do nothing. A paper tiger is a threat you personally are not worried about, often because you own that area and believe it is under control (S005, S013). An elephant is what you fear the team is not saying, and it may not be a threat so much as a silence (S013). Agenda he publishes: about an hour, quiet writing of at least two tigers each, quiet voting (up to five votes, so people must be selective), then an action plan (S005, S013). The plan keeps the top threats in the speaker's words, gives each theme one owner (a DRI), and is tracked for the rest of the project (S013).
- **When to use:** Major launches and initiatives, about one to three months before launch, including a meaningful change to terms of service (S005, S037). Include every function involved. Split a large launch into separate sessions if needed (S001). He also still wants a post-mortem when the outcome was good (S013).
- **How to score:** The meeting is the easy part. Score the action plan: top 3 to 5 tigers and elephants, each with one owner, not a promise to fix everything (S005). Fixing every problem stalls the work. Minor problems are ones you explicitly live with (S013). He says the practice made launches calmer and was not mistake-free (S005). About an hour or two of meeting time is the cost he cites (S037).
- **Quote:** "Tiger - A clear threat that will hurt us if we don’t do something about it." (S005)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S005, S008, S009, S012, S013, S017, S023, S037)

## 5. Impact = (Execution ^ Strategy) x Market
- **What it is:** A heuristic, not a formula, for which factor is actually deciding the outcome. In 2020 he first contrasts a convenient illusion, inputs plus outputs equals outcomes, with an inconvenient one, inputs plus outputs multiplied by market, and then offers execution raised to strategy, times market (S009). The newer write-up is 22 August 2021, under the name Impact (S028).
- **Parts:** Set each factor to zero. Strategy at zero leaves impact about equal to the market: a very bad strategy will not kill you, and it will cap execution until you fix it. Execution at zero leaves impact about zero, so he would rather have some execution and no strategy than the reverse. Market at zero, or a market that is shrinking fast, also leaves impact about zero (S028). Strategy still has an exponential effect, so excellent strategy with merely OK execution beats excellent execution with merely OK strategy (S028). Definitions in that thread: strategy is the choices that differentiate and distribute the product into a long-term advantage, tied to a credible plan; execution is pace and quality (S028). A 2020 shorthand, Market above Strategy above Execution, is the coarser cousin (S029). The 2021 thread is the one to use when they seem to disagree.
- **When to use:** A founder is sure the problem is only execution, or sure that strategy talk is the whole job. Also when picking a market that may not be there.
- **How to score:** Both slogans fail. "Execution is everything, Strategy is only for MBAs" (S028) "Strategy is everything, Execution is for losers" (S028) Older view, roughly 2008 to 2014 at Google: strategy is useless and execution is where it is at. He dates the change to about 2014 at Twitter, and in the 31 October 2024 talk he calls the old view wrong, including for early products at Stripe (S002). Newer working rule, inferred from S001, S002, S028: keep a real strategy even when the product is early, hire a new team for execution first because that early culture is hard to undo, and once a reasonable hypothesis exists, put the team's effort into execution.
- **Quote:** "So I’d rather have excellent strategy & just OK execution vs. excellent execution & just OK strategy." (S028)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S009, S028, S029, S045, S047)

## 6. Product sense, taste, and the 10-30-50 PM
- **What it is:** The skill for the calls you cannot A/B. Product sense, as he defines it, is empathy, domain knowledge, and creativity (S011). Taste is calling what is good before the result is in, with the social proof and the authority proof stripped off (S002).
- **Parts:** A 10-30-50 PM is top 10 percent in one of product sense, analytical sense, and execution sense, top 30 percent in a second, and top 50 percent in the third. The order does not matter (S011, S021). You build the weak sense by choosing the next project for that sense (S011). Empathy is the piece he singles out as the most valuable product skill (S018). In 2023 he says the cocky version is usually someone with decent natural sense and little real-world experience, not someone with the strongest product sense (S037).
- **When to use:** Hiring a PM, judging a bet that data cannot settle, and checking whether a fashionable idea is being adopted because it is good or because a famous person said it (S002).
- **How to score:** Investing in product sense is among the highest-ROI career moves he names (S011). A harsh grade after the outcome is known takes no taste. Saying a winner is a genius in 2024 takes no taste (S002). We tend to judge user need well, market size so-so, and differentiation poorly (S022).
- **Quote:** "taste is about the ability to identify what is really good, without needing to see its results" (S002)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S011, S012, S018, S021, S027, S037)

## 7. Execution problems in disguise
- **What it is:** In a high-performing team with good intentions, the thing called an execution problem is usually a strategy problem, an interpersonal problem, or a culture problem. The test he uses: if you put on a bandage and the bandage falls, you are not looking at a local execution bug (S001).
- **Parts:** Strategy version: nobody can say the strategy, so priorities drift and a new review meeting will not fix it (S001, S019). Culture version: people are rewarded only for their own OKRs, so helping another team is punished (S001). Interpersonal version: two managers do not get along. Real execution problems exist too: funding, skill gaps, tools, org structure, process, dependencies, technical complexity, coordination complexity (S029). Resource complaints are often a symptom (S029). Easy problems first, and staying there, is a pattern he calls a silent killer of products (S029).
- **When to use:** The team is overwhelmed, misaligned, or asking for another sync, another process, or more people. Also when a leader says the strategy is fine and the team only needs to prioritize, but cannot state the strategy without rambling (S029).
- **How to score:** If a fix has to be reapplied, stop adding process and name the upstream cause (S001, S019). If the team repeatedly fails at prioritization, the strategy is missing or unspoken (S029). Do not use this to deny a true skills gap or a broken system (S001, S029). Bad leaders live on bandages (S019).
- **Quote:** "In the majority of cases though, what is initially expressed as an Execution problem isn't an Execution problem at all." (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S002, S015, S019, S029, S048)

## 8. Apple Pie Positions
- **What it is:** A sentence that makes the speaker sound good and is hard for anyone else to push back on, so the room nods. He started using the name around 2020 (S014, S030) and tightened the definition on 23 February 2023 (S050). The 2023 statement is the newer one.
- **Parts:** The February 2020 list opens with "We need more scalable processes." (S030) and goes on to weekly check-ins, aligned incentives, better metrics before deciding, a better go-to-market, an offsite, a re-org, mission language, and shipping-velocity talk that blocks a small delay (S030). He later marks the hunt for product-market fit as one of these sentences: "trying to find Product-Market fit" (S024). Bad strategy often hides inside this language and reads like a prayer (S035).
- **When to use:** A planning meeting, a product review, or a hiring pitch where everyone agrees too fast. Also when a success-criteria question or a data question is being used to stall.
- **How to score:** The same sentence can be the right focus or an apple pie line. Context decides, and he says no tweet replaces judgment (S050). A nod is a fail if the personal risk of disagreeing is the reason for the nod (S014). Shipping-velocity talk fails when it blocks a small delay that would prevent a larger harm (S030).
- **Quote:** "A statement that instantly elevates the person who is saying it and is simultaneously hard for anyone else to push back on" (S050)
- **Sources:** 6 (S014, S023, S024, S030, S035, S050)

## 9. The 7 biases of product teams
- **What it is:** A 2020 list of why products fail even when the team can build. He says it took years of watching, and that companies fail at the decision, not because the plan looked bad. He tells a team to adopt only the antidotes for the biases it actually has (S008).
- **Parts:** Focusing illusion (a customer asked about one problem crowds out bigger ones; the fix is a Customer Problems Stack Rank). IKEA effect (attachment to what you already built, especially a product that is neither failing nor winning). Build-now bias (rush the spec to unblock engineers; a promise to repair it in a later version rarely ends well). Execution orientation fallacy (choose what is easy to execute today). Maslow's hammer (process, data, or a past playbook used regardless of context). Russian roulette (ignore a scenario that could wreck the product or the company's reputation; a pre-mortem is his antidote). Authority approval bias (shape the plan so the review succeeds, or so an exec's belief is confirmed). Antidotes he pairs with them: deep customer empathy, first-principles thinking, velocity as speed in the right direction, high ambition with high agency, context before tools, preventing catastrophe ahead of heroics, constructive dissonance (S008, S034). Customer Problems Stack Rank is his test: after a customer says a problem matters, ask them to rank it against the other problems they are actually solving (S008, S024).
- **When to use:** A team is about to staff a B2B idea customers praised, a review is being optimized for the executive's reaction, or a struggling product is getting another quarter because of the work already sunk.
- **How to score:** If customers will not rank the problem near the top, do not build it (S008, S024). If the plan is the one you can ship easily, or the one that will make the review comfortable, the decision is already bent (S008, S034). He says no single sign proves a bias, and that data and process are not themselves bad (S008). Much of the list, he says, reduces to a line he heard from Mike Seibel: "don't be too smart" (S008).
- **Quote:** "we are so focused on execution that we craft flawed product strategies and make suboptimal product decisions because we base these decisions on what we know we can easily execute today." (S008)
- **Sources:** 5 (S008, S012, S023, S024, S034)

## 10. Preventable Problem Paradox
- **What it is:** Organizations reward the person who solves a problem more than the person who stops it happening, so they keep creating problems to solve. His picture is the Niagara Falls scene in Superman II: the warning is ignored, the rescue gets the applause (S017). He presents the pre-mortem as the most practical of a few counters (S013).
- **Parts:** Three moves for a leader: make the pattern visible, change rewards and recognition, and use pre-mortems (S017). Related rule from the good-PM thread: be discerning about which problems to prevent, which to solve, and which not to solve (S015). Solving one problem often creates another, so management's job is to pick the problems it will live with (S013, S029).
- **When to use:** The company celebrates firefighting, a launch hero, or a rescue, and the same class of failure keeps returning. Less fitting at the very start of a career, where he says you should still go solve the problem in front of the team (S017).
- **How to score:** If appreciation is the motive, bet that people will choose the rescue over the prevention (S017). A leader who is past the early stage and still lives on the problem of the day is failing this test. The iceberg avoided is the win, even though it produces no story (S017).
- **Quote:** "Organizations are predisposed to creating & solving problems rather than preventing them in the first place." (S023)
- **Sources:** 5 (S013, S015, S017, S021, S023)

## 11. Sensible null hypothesis
- **What it is:** Start from the assumption that the product will not be wanted, and make the case prove itself. He uses one version for B2B and a harsher one for consumer.
- **Parts:** B2B null: the customer does not really need it, will not pay, and will not deploy or use it (S012, S053). Four questions he wants answered before a B2B build: is the problem acute, why you rather than the alternatives, why they would change an ingrained habit, and whether those answers hold for enough customers (S053). The default alternative is inaction, and a spreadsheet is often next (S053). Kryptonite he lists: inertia, spreadsheets, email and Slack workflows, security, deployment delays, re-orgs, budget cuts (S053). Consumer null: they will not invite anyone, will sign in through password reset every time, would rather be on TikTok or YouTube or Netflix, will never find the feature that took two years, will refuse notifications, ignore email, skip the upgrade, let the subscription lapse, and switch to a shinier rival (S052). None of that is the user's fault (S052).
- **When to use:** Before staffing a feature or a new product, especially when interviews are being used to confirm an idea or an exec mandate (S024). Also when a failed B2B product is about to get a listening tour and another year.
- **How to score:** Excitement in an interview is not a purchase. If the team cannot say why the customer would move off inaction, the bet fails (S053). A product that customers liked in the room and then did not adopt should have been caught by a stack rank, and often should not have been built (S008, S024). Consumer work is harder than B2B on this test, in his words (S053).
- **Quote:** "A sensible null hypothesis for B2B products is that the customer doesn’t really need your product, will not pay for it, and will not deploy or use it." (S012)
- **Sources:** 4 (S012, S034, S052, S053)

## 12. Explicit focus limits
- **What it is:** A small number of things you will do flawlessly, and an explicit no for the rest. He uses three related tools: a personal and company cap, a six-month focus checklist, and, for a tiny fast-growing team, Derek Sivers' hell-yeah-or-no rule, which Doshi did not invent (S027).
- **Parts:** Rule of 3, 5, 7: about three things done flawlessly as an individual, five as a team, seven as a company, on the scale of a day, a week, a month, or a year. Flaws in the rest are acceptable (S011). Checklist for an overwhelmed product leader: the most important thing for the product over the next six months, whether you are sure, what share of your time goes to it, and why you are afraid to focus more (S010). A 2022 companion asks which few actions truly matter, what blocks them, whether the block is inside your control, and what lie you just told yourself (S054). Hell yeah or no: if it is not a clear yes, it is a no, and the skill is in reaching the yes. He limits that rule to a fast-growing product with a tiny team (S027).
- **When to use:** A plan that is a pile of reasonable projects, a founder carrying twelve priorities, or a small team adopting a heavy scoring spreadsheet.
- **How to score:** More than about three flawless personal commitments, or more than one or two company-level big bets in the protected slice of time, fails the cap (S001, S011). A team that cannot name the six-month thing, or that flinches at the fear question, is still on the ROI treadmill (S010). Hell yeah is the wrong tool once the team is large enough to need themes and a strategy. He says there are other valid ways to prioritize and no universal rules (S019).
- **Quote:** "As individuals, there are about 3 things that we should do flawlessly on any given day, week, month, year." (S011)
- **Sources:** 4 (S010, S011, S027, S054)

## 13. Radical Delegation
- **What it is:** What he says to do when the Eisenhower matrix stops working. Start from the tasks above the line, delegate most of them with a delegation contract, keep a smaller set, and use the handoff to raise the team's skill (S010). He repeats Munger's description of the Berkshire system, delegation just short of abdication, as the spirit, and that line is Munger's (S010).
- **Parts:** Aims he lists: one singular impact, empowerment, stretch work, flow, and less burnout (S010, S036). In 2023 he calls it the change that mattered for him as a PM leader, the way LNO mattered for him as a senior IC (S037). His own Slack policy is an instance: a few channels, mute before deep work, complex talk moved to a doc, contentious talk moved to a call, no fear of missing the rest, because his singular value was deep product work, 1:1s, and coaching through this kind of delegation (S044).
- **When to use:** A leader whose calendar is full of work other people could own, or who is still doing proof-of-worth tasks. He aims it at senior people. Early-career brute force, he says, works and then stops (S010).
- **How to score:** If the leader's week is still the important-and-urgent quadrant, delegation has not started. The test is a smaller personal set and a team that is stretching, not a leader who vanished. He says the spirit is not for everyone, and that some weeks it is impossible (S010). Someone whose singular value is constant responsiveness may need the opposite Slack policy, and will then be unable to scale past a point (S044).
- **Quote:** "That leaves you with a smaller subset for your focus and the delegation helps raise your broader team's skills." (S010)
- **Sources:** 4 (S010, S036, S037, S044)

## 14. Metrics that inform
- **What it is:** A small set of numbers that tell you whether the strategy is working, used as an input to judgment. He wants teams metrics-informed. A metrics-driven team lets the number decide.
- **Parts:** Six categories: health, usage, adoption, satisfaction, ecosystem, outcome. Skip a category on purpose if you must (S008, S016). Key metrics: 3 to 5, each responsive to product changes, an aggregate of user value, tied to business value, and meant to last at least two to three years. Add 3 to 5 leading metrics when the result lags (S016). Inputs to a product decision, in his list: data, customer feedback, technical effort, strategy, industry landscape, instinct. Heavy on the first three and you move fast and stay incremental. Heavy on the last three and you can make a large bet and also fail early. He wants a balance (S016). For a B2B buyer he also asks whether the product keeps them from being fired, helps them get promoted, or directly earns revenue (S008). False precision on targets is a trap (S016, S043).
- **When to use:** Choosing the scoreboard for a bet, a business review, or a dashboard the team will stare at every morning. He says to treat the dashboard like a product (S016).
- **How to score:** A key-metric list longer than about five, or a metric you contort so the story works, fails (S016). Gaming the number is a firing offense for a manager (S009, S016). "metrics made me do it" is not a defense for a leader (S008). If Sales cannot sell it, it is still the PM's problem (S016). SQL beyond the first couple of years is low leverage. Drawing a decision from the data is a top skill at every level (S016).
- **Quote:** "Good PMs make metrics-driven product decisions. Great PMs make metrics-informed product decisions." (S015)
- **Sources:** 4 (S008, S009, S015, S016)

## 15. Opportunity cost over ROI
- **What it is:** In a high-leverage role, hundreds of tasks have positive ROI, so positive ROI is the wrong bar. ROI puts time in the denominator and pulls you toward quick, cheap work. Opportunity cost is the value of the best option minus the value of the option you picked, so the question becomes whether this is the best use of the time (S001, S010, S036).
- **Parts:** He does not want this turned into a spreadsheet column. "I do not recommend trying to quantify opportunity costs because it's a lost cost." (S001) The move is a shift in which work you even consider. Quick wins are allowed. They must not be the whole plate (S001). Fear of the high-leverage task, often strategy, is a signal to schedule the work, which he says in both the 2022 conversation and the 2024 essay (S001, S003, S010).
- **When to use:** Quarterly planning, a founder's week, or any list where every item can be justified. Pair it with an explicit time split so the big bet has a protected share (see the allocation framework).
- **How to score:** A plan made of sure, small, positive-ROI items, with the ambiguous bets left off, fails even if every line "pays back" (S001, S036). A plan that can name the best option it is refusing scores well. Do not invent a numeric opportunity-cost column (S001). From his Google ads years: an unwritten bar was that under $100 million was not worth the discussion, because billion-dollar bets existed. The habit he kept was asking whether a $1 million bet can become $10 million and a $10 million bet $100 million. Inferred from S001: do not copy the dollar cutoff. He says that high bar made Google miss trends.
- **Quote:** "Opportunity cost is simply the value of the optimal option minus the value of the chosen option." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S010, S036)

## 16. Time allocation by theme
- **What it is:** After the strategy is clear, the leader sets a rough split of team time across a few themes and leaves most themes at zero for that period. The percentages are examples. He tells each team to pick its own (S001, S019).
- **Parts:** His planning example, adapted from Google's 70 percent search and ads, 20 percent apps, 10 percent other bets: about 60 percent incrementals that improve users' days, 30 percent big new initiatives, 10 percent stability and infrastructure (S001, S019, S043). Because the big slice is 30 percent, it holds one or two initiatives, not five (S001). A quarter he actually ran: 60 percent differentiators, 30 percent tech foundation, 10 percent speculative bets (S019). A menu of themes he offers for a single product: differentiators, table stakes, incrementals, embarrassments (broken windows), customer specials, speculative bets, tech foundation. In a given quarter you rarely fund all of them (S019). Another cut he uses when judging a portfolio: offense or defense, explore or exploit, growth or protect or foundation, and whether the work depends on another team (S019).
- **When to use:** A quarterly or annual plan, once strategy exists. The leader sets themes and the split. Teams propose. A scoring sheet such as RICE can draw a cut line. It does not pick the themes (S019).
- **How to score:** If the 30 percent slice contains five large bets, it is still a quick-win plan (S001). If every theme has a share, the split is fake (S019). Tech and infrastructure budget is a floor, not a ceiling (S022). Judgment remains after the sheet (S019). He still does some resource allocation. He drops false precision and templates that do not make sense (S002, S043).
- **Quote:** "And because it's 30%, it can't be five big new initiatives, it's probably one or two." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S019, S043)

## 17. Eigenquestion and WAYRTTD
- **What it is:** Two question tools for a decision that is about to get lost in options. WAYRTTD (What Are You Really Trying To Do) is the first thinking tool he says he learned at Stripe (S008). The eigenquestion is Shishir Mehrotra's, not his: the one question which, once answered, settles most of the other questions on that decision (S008, S016, S051).
- **Parts:** He now spends about half the decision time making sure the question is right, and half on pros and cons (S051). His Netflix example, squeezed into a short post: do we care more about catalog size or about consumption friction (S051). The eigenquestion is also how he forces the choice to line up with vision and strategy. A typical meeting that starts with metrics, then three options, then a recommendation, litigates the slides instead (S051).
- **When to use:** A high-stakes, messy choice: pricing structure, a platform bet, a kill-or-continue. Not for a routine ticket.
- **How to score:** If the room cannot say the one question, it is not ready to pick an option. The Netflix-style question should make several downstream arguments unnecessary. He says the detour feels slow and is faster (S051). Combine the two tools, and still use judgment (S051).
- **Quote:** "The Eigenquestion is the question, which, if answered, provides the basis for answering most other substantial questions associated with this decision." (S016)
- **Sources:** 3 (S008, S016, S051)

## 18. Good PM, Great PM
- **What it is:** A long 2020 contrast he later tells people to reread for a few months. Good is consistent delivery. Great is a singular improvement in the company's trajectory. He says nobody does all of it all the time, and the lines are signposts, not commandments (S015, S038).
- **Parts:** The pairs that bear on focus: metrics-driven versus metrics-informed; problem solver versus problem preventer; optimizing a given strategy versus first making sure the strategy is optimal; answers and data versus questions and wisdom; stakeholders as approvers versus advisors; the team's goals versus the company's good, including giving up the team's targets when the company needs it (S015). Great also means the team can say the strategy on an elevator ride, colleagues can push back easily, and the question asked is "what am I getting wrong?" (S015). Docs, the API, the blog, the site, and the support email are part of the product (S015). He wants product-obsessed companies, where eng, design, PM, and data science are peers, over PM-driven companies (S015, S021).
- **When to use:** Coaching a PM who is busy and effective, a promotion discussion, or a founder who thinks the job is shipping the roadmap they were handed.
- **How to score:** Impeccable execution of a flawed strategy fails, because the team's time was the scarce thing (S015). A PM who is rarely overwhelmed, who can argue the opposite of their own view, and who shares a failure with the company, scores toward great (S015). Start with a few of the lines. He says he knows no great PM who hits every one (S015).
- **Quote:** "Great PMs know that their team’s time is too precious to be squandered away on impeccable execution of a flawed strategy." (S015)
- **Sources:** 3 (S008, S015, S038)

## 19. Three levels of product work (impact, execution, optics)
- **What it is:** A shared vocabulary for a fight that is really about altitude. Impact is the effect on customers, the brand, and the business. Execution is getting it done. Optics is creating awareness of the impact and the execution (S001, S002).
- **Parts:** Conflict between smart people is often a mismatch of level. His example is a product review: the PM is explaining execution, the CEO is asking about impact (S001). Everyone has a default. His is impact. Senior jobs in a large company force optics, and willpower is finite, so around a team of about 50 he stepped back to earlier-stage product (S002). No level is always right. Early teams, once they have a reasonable hypothesis, should lean on execution. A platform team with outages may need more optics (S001). Optics becomes harmful when hiring, firing, promotion, and all-hands praise make it the goal (S001). A 2024 cousin, the Content-Confidence-Context model, is his name for how work is perceived in mid-size and large companies. The fetched post names the model and does not spell the three parts out (S045).
- **When to use:** A recurring argument in review, a founder who feels the job has become performance, or a culture that rewards the appearance of progress.
- **How to score:** Name the level out loud before debating the issue. If optics is what gets promoted, the product will drift toward what photographs well (S001). If a leader's default level and the job's required level stay misaligned, the frustration is structural. He treats that as a reason to change the job, not as a cue to spend more willpower (S002).
- **Quote:** "This idea that there are three levels of product work, impact, execution, and optics." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S002)

## 20. Three checks before you build
- **What it is:** A pause, then three questions, before a feature gets a yes. Thinking is cheap, so he wants more of it, not less (S002). In 2020 the three product questions are: does it serve an implicit or explicit user need very well, is it differentiated enough, and is the market large enough. He says teams judge the need well, the market so-so, and differentiation poorly (S022). In 2024 he states the pre-build check as customer motivation, differentiation, and distribution (S002).
- **Parts:** Pause for two minutes, two days, or two weeks (S002). Be opinionated about the target segment. A thing that resonates hard for a small segment beats a thing aimed at everyone (S022). Marketing has its own three questions he wants the product to survive: what does it do, how good is it, is it for me (S022). On doors: he rejects the casual reading of two-way doors for PM leaders. At Bezos's level many bets are reversible. At the PM's level the feature brings review scrutiny, table-stakes follow-ons, and debt, so the door was one-way (S002). He also warns that people adopt the door metaphor for the catchy image and the authority, not because they tested the idea (S002).
- **When to use:** The room is about to approve a feature because an engineer is free, because a stakeholder asked, or because someone called it a two-way door (S002).
- **How to score:** If motivation, differentiation, and distribution are still fuzzy, the answer is not yet. If the feature's main future is a trail of follow-on work, it should not have been built, and the busyness is self-inflicted (S002). A first solution on a high-stakes problem is not enough (S022). Backtracking later is harder than it sounds, because of ego, user attachment, and code that does not want to move (S022).
- **Quote:** "most doors that look like two-way doors are actually one-way doors." (S002)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S022)

## 21. Agreed Target Quality
- **What it is:** A shared label for the quality bar of a milestone, so engineering, design, and PM can argue about the target instead of about each other's taste. He learned it from Jeff Seibert at Twitter and does not claim the original task-quality idea (S021). He also applies the LNO instinct to features: be intentional about the level you are building (S012).
- **Parts:** The useful part, he says, is the common lexicon (S021). Once the level is agreed, disagreements get sharper and smaller (S012). It is the feature-level cousin of LNO: some things deserve perfection, some deserve less, and the team should say which before the work starts.
- **When to use:** A milestone where one function wants polish and another wants it shipped, or where a call for high quality is being used as an apple pie line.
- **How to score:** If the team cannot name the quality target, the debate is not ready. Agreement on the target should make the remaining argument about whether the work hits it (S012, S021).
- **Quote:** "The most useful part of the Agreed Target Quality framework is the common lexicon." (S021)
- **Sources:** 2 (S012, S021)

## 22. Proof of Worth
- **What it is:** Work a PM does to prove they deserve a seat: SQL, a bug fix, wireframes, perfect notes, instant replies, a support queue. Fine, even useful, early on a new team. Harmful once it is still the job (S010, S033).
- **Parts:** He marks it a yellow flag if it is still expected past the junior levels, and a red flag if the ladder requires it at Group PM or above. The response he wants then is to edit the ladder or leave (S033). The main job is defining products that will be successful, not collecting peer approval (S003, S033). A stressed mid-career PM, in his test, is usually someone who does not see task leverage and is still doing this work (S033).
- **When to use:** A senior PM's calendar is full of specialist tasks the company already employs specialists to do, or a founder is grading PMs on responsiveness and query volume.
- **How to score:** Early-career, occasional, and intentional: acceptable. Required of a senior PM: fail. Even a strong PM may still choose a few of these tasks, on their own terms (S033). He tells leaders not to require them of themselves or of senior PMs (S010).
- **Quote:** "Understand the dangers of Proof of Worth tasks. If you do them, do them sparingly and intentionally." (S010)
- **Sources:** 2 (S010, S033)

## 23. Insight, Execution, Impact (PM evaluation)
- **What it is:** The review he used in the second half of his time at Stripe, instead of a ladder that ignores what stage the product is in. He points to Kent Beck's 3X for the stages (new initiative, growth, mature cash cow, a declining product that needs a turnaround) and does not claim that model (S006).
- **Parts:** Questions cover how well the PM understands the product, users, and domain; how that becomes strategy, goals, and a plan; whether P0 and P1 ships landed; collaboration; the quality bar; business impact through leading indicators (beta adoption, satisfaction, major deals, usage growth) and lagging ones (revenue, user growth, margin, cost); and whether the team, org, or company got better (S006). Agree up front what progress matters over the next 3, 6, and 9 months, and what evidence will count. Give concrete gaps and the evidence you need to see (S006).
- **When to use:** A performance or career conversation, and any time a company is grading an early-product PM with a mature-product ladder.
- **How to score:** A ladder that pays for moving a metric by a few basis points, and that punishes risk on an ambiguous early product, fails his test (S006). The stage comes first. The same behavior is a different job on a new bet than on a cash cow. Inputs mix judgment and evidence, and he says to adapt to the company (S006).
- **Quote:** "A key part of this framework is to recognize the stage of the product that the PM is working on, as the PM role, skillsets, and capabilities needed are very different across product stages." (S006)
- **Sources:** 1 (S006)

## 24. Product leader archetypes (Operator, Craftsperson, Visionary)
- **What it is:** Three hats, plus a self-check, for who should lead a product bet. The essay date in the source table is 31 July 2025. Older view, stated as dropped in that essay: "I used to think product leadership was all about making great product decisions and building self-managing teams that can create successful products." (S007) Newer view: most leaders lean Operator, Craftsperson, or Visionary, each with a blind spot, and you sometimes have to lead as an Operator even if that is not your hat (S007).
- **Parts:** Operators grow teams, align groups, and communicate, and they often rely on others for deep product knowledge. Craftspeople stay close to the team, set strategy carefully, mentor, and respect research, and they struggle with company process without an execution partner. Visionaries see the new bet and inspire, are hard to manage early, and need both of the other two to make the vision real (S007). Use the set to choose your role, hire a CPO, and build a leadership group that covers the holes.
- **When to use:** Hiring a product leader, pairing a founder with a complement, or noticing that a visionary is being graded as an operator (or the reverse).
- **How to score:** A leadership team of one hat fails. A visionary without an operator and a craftsperson is a vision that will not ship (S007). Knowing your own hat comes before the role decision. He is explicit that each style has a blind spot (S007).
- **Quote:** "most people tend to fall into one of three leadership styles: Operator, Craftsperson, or Visionary." (S007)
- **Sources:** 1 (S007)
