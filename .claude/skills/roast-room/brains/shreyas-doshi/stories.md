# Stories: Shreyas Doshi

16 stories, built from the verified notes in batch-01, batch-02, and batch-03. The cases he uses when the question is which bet deserves focus, and what to leave undone.

1. **The Google ads $100 million bar.** On the ads team, an opportunity under about $100 million was not worth the meeting, because billion-dollar bets were available. He kept the habit of asking how to make a bet much bigger, and of dropping the smaller ones that blocked it. Lesson: a high bar creates focus, and it can also miss a trend that does not yet look big (S001).

2. **Three overwhelmed years at Google.** From 2008, his first three years as a PM were long hours, bad sleep, and a to-do list he could not dent, even while he was promoted. A piece on task prioritization led him, over about a year, to sort work into leverage, neutral, and overhead. Lesson: equal effort on unequal tasks is what made the list endless (S001, S003, S036).

3. **Stripe Connect and the scary payments bet.** He joined a team busy with many positive-ROI items, heard a customer need for more flexible marketplace payments, and pushed a big ambiguous project the positive-ROI list would have skipped. It turned into a huge opportunity. Lesson: the bet that changes the trajectory is easy to crowd out with sure small wins (S001).

4. **Five sure things and two ambiguous ones.** In quarterly planning he kept seeing five well-defined items with small or medium impact, and two unclear bets that might change the business. The plan filled with the five. Lesson: positive ROI is a comfortable way to avoid the bet you are afraid to flesh out (S001).

5. **Jack's question in the acquisition room.** He was on the fence about something his team might acquire. The room had done the pros, cons, strategy, and metrics. Jack Dorsey asked only how it would make users love Twitter more, and he had no answer. Lesson: a proxy debate can hide the only question that mattered (S001).

6. **YouTube accounts moved into Google accounts.** The migration caused a real backlash. Later, nobody remembered, and people happily used YouTube. He had seen the same pattern in identity work at Yahoo. Lesson: the right long-term call can hurt now, and the hurt is a bad reason to undo it (S001).

7. **Pre-mortems at Stripe.** He ran them on major launches, including Stripe Connect and Stripe Terminal. The standard exercise was lively in the room and then forgotten, so he added tigers, paper tigers, and elephants. Other teams picked up the words, and people raised their own tigers in meetings he was not in. Lesson: name the few threats that can kill the launch, give each an owner, and leave the minor ones alone (S001, S005, S013).

8. **Strategy is for MBAs, then Twitter.** Early at Google he repeated that execution was everything and strategy was for other people. A peer asked for his strategy and he brushed it off. At Twitter around 2014, after six to nine months, he decided the company had the product, the network, the brand, and the talent, and still lacked a real product strategy. He later said he had been wrong, and early products at Stripe made the point sharper. Lesson: a great asset does not replace the choices about where to play and how to win (S002).

9. **Stripe Connect planned in three days.** Annual planning around him often took four to six weeks and was forgotten by late February. With a strategy people were already aligned on, his plan for Stripe Connect took about three days. Over ten years he spent about a quarter of the time other managers spent on planning, and the teams still shipped. Lesson: the long planning cycle is what you do when the strategy is missing (S002, S043).

10. **The two-way door that was not.** A feature gets approved as reversible, ships, shows weak adoption at the business review, and still does not help sales. The team adds the table-stakes follow-ons and leaves busier than before. Lesson: for the person who inherits the follow-on work, most two-way doors are one-way doors, which is why the team is busy (S002).

11. **The team of about 50.** At Stripe, once his org was about 50 people including engineers, the job had become mostly optics. Willpower could force it for a while. He went back to an earlier-stage product. Lesson: past a certain size the leader's hours go to being seen, and that is a real reason to refuse the bigger job if your strength is the product itself (S002).

12. **Andy, Kris, and Jo.** Customers said the problem mattered. The team shipped a v1 in about six months, got no adoption, added features for another six months, reworked go-to-market for about a year, and shut it down after almost three years. The customers who had said yes could not adopt it, because that one problem had crowded out bigger ones. He tells the same arc as a B2B saga: excitement, a pot-committed team, a new PM, another listening tour, sunset. Lesson: a customer yes is not a priority. Make them stack rank the problem before you build (S008, S012, S024).

13. **The weekend PRD.** Under pressure that engineers were blocked, a PM spent the weekend finishing requirements and promised to fix the research in v2. Sunk cost and attachment to the work already done then kept the weak product alive. Lesson: the weeks you refuse to spend up front become years on the wrong thing, and v2 rarely saves it (S008, S012).

14. **The boy at Niagara Falls.** In the Superman II scene, Clark Kent tries to stop the boy falling and is ignored. Superman catches him and gets the applause. Lesson: if people work for appreciation, they will let the problem happen and then rescue it. Change what the company applauds, or you will keep buying heroics (S017).

15. **Our strategy is solid.** A leader says the team only needs to prioritize better. Asked what the strategy is, they ramble for several minutes. Lesson: a prioritization failure that repeats is usually a strategy nobody can say (S029).

16. **The OKR death spiral.** The company starts rewarding people who hit every OKR. People set OKRs they can hit. Shipping gets slower and worse, the metrics get worse, and every OKR is green. Lesson: a scoreboard that pays for completion will pull the team off the bet that could actually move the business (S045, S001).
