# Frameworks: Marty Cagan

20 frameworks, most-used first, built from the verified notes on 50 sources (S001 to S050). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. How-to-score lines are scoring guidance from his thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. The four big risks
- **What it is:** The list he runs before anyone builds. From the first edition of INSPIRED (2008) through his 2011 definition of a minimum viable product, the list was three: value, usability, and feasibility. Value was supposed to cover both the customer and the business (S006, S042). Newer, from the second edition, which he is already teaching on 2017-12-04 (S021) and dates to 2018 (S042): four risks, with business viability named on its own, because teams kept the customer and skipped the business. He still says three is easier to hold in your head, and that four is worth the extra load. Use the four.
- **Parts:** Value: will a customer buy it or a user choose it. Usability: can they figure it out. Feasibility: can engineers build it with the time, skills, and technology on hand. Business viability: does it work for sales, marketing, finance, legal, compliance, and the brand. The product manager owns value and viability and is accountable for the outcome. The designer owns usability. The tech lead owns feasibility. They discover the solution together.
- **When to use:** Any bet on what to build, including a founder's favorite idea, a sales request, and a roadmap item. Run it before production code. Also the test of whether a "finished" solution is actually finished.
- **How to score:** Guidance: value is usually the toughest risk (S021). A pass on usability is not a pass on value. Customer love without a business that can sell, fund, service, and comply is not a pass (S021, S036, S042). Assess severity and consequence for each risk. Minor risks go on the backlog. Major risks get a test (S032). Treating every risk as major is a fail, because the real risks then get no time (S032). No taxonomy at all is how efforts fail (S042).
- **Quote:** "Assessing the product risks is the first thing we do when we're trying to discover a solution worth building." (S042)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S006, S010, S018, S019, S021, S025, S031, S032, S035, S036, S042, S044, S045, S048)

## 2. Empowered product team, feature team, delivery team
- **What it is:** His test for what kind of team is actually in the room. A delivery team is developers plus a product owner he calls a backlog administrator, and he treats SAFe as this model. A feature team is handed a prioritized roadmap of output and serves the business. An empowered product team is cross-functional, assigned problems, and measured on outcomes. The explicit three-way split is 2019 (S025). The 2014 good-team / bad-team list is the same contrast in earlier clothes (S014).
- **Parts:** Who decides the solution. Who owns value and viability (the team, or the stakeholder who asked for the feature). Whether the measure is a date and a release, or a business result. Whether the people are missionaries with a vision or mercenaries taking orders.
- **When to use:** Before arguing technique. Discovery advice applied to a feature team or a delivery team misses, because those teams are not allowed to change the solution. Also the test when someone asks for a process, a certification, or a transformation that only renames roles.
- **How to score:** Guidance: a roadmap of features and dates is a feature team. A backlog administrator is a delivery team. Problems plus business outcomes, with the team free to pick the solution, is empowered (S025). If a stakeholder still owns value and viability, the titles do not matter (S025). An empowered team can do everything a feature team can do, and more (S001). He admits that even strong companies keep some feature teams until trust is earned, or because a leader wants to dictate the solution (S025).
- **Quote:** "Are you provided roadmaps with prioritized features and dates, or are you assigned problems to solve with business outcomes?" (S025)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S014, S023, S025, S030, S035, S036, S045, S046, S048, S049)

## 3. Product discovery (continuous discovery and continuous delivery)
- **What it is:** How a team finds a solution worth building, in parallel with delivery, not as a phase before it. In 2012 he called this Dual-Track Agile: discovery makes validated backlog items, delivery makes releasable software (S009). He had already dropped the earlier name Discovery Sprints, because it implied a time box and a sequence. Newer, from the second edition of INSPIRED: he stopped saying Dual-Track Agile, because the name made people stare at process. The current names are continuous discovery and continuous delivery (S009, S019).
- **Parts:** The product manager, designer, and lead engineer work side by side. They tackle value, usability, feasibility, and viability before production code. They validate the fastest, cheapest way, usually with a prototype. The same team is accountable for discovery and delivery, and measures itself on business results. One or two hard problems a quarter, plus keep-the-lights-on work.
- **When to use:** The team is about to build, is stuck in sprint planning, or is calling a usability pass "discovery." Also when a founder asks if the idea is real. A strong early founder is the exception he names: with a dominant founder, technique almost does not matter if the founder is good (S001).
- **How to score:** Guidance: at least half of ideas will not work, and the ones that can work need several iterations (S011). Days, not months (S011). Building and launching is the slowest, most expensive test (S009). A/B tests on small tweaks are optimization, not discovery (S001). Testing everything is also a fail: the team goes nowhere (S018). If nothing is risky, skip discovery and put the work on the backlog (S032).
- **Quote:** "Product discovery is about rapidly experimenting, mostly using prototypes, to try to come up with a solution that solves for all of the constraints and achieves the necessary outcome." (S045)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S006, S009, S011, S013, S018, S019, S031, S044, S045)

## 4. The product operating model
- **What it is:** His name, from 2023 to 2024, for how the best product companies decide, discover, and deliver. He did not invent it. He says it is the principles those companies share, about 20 of them (S001, S050). The 2017 argument was already "a model, not a process," with discovery and delivery in parallel (S019). Newer: the product operating model adds product strategy as a third dimension and is what TRANSFORMED is about (S040, S044, S045).
- **Parts:** Three dimensions: which problems to solve (product strategy), how you solve them (product discovery), how you build, test, and deploy (product delivery). Five concepts: product culture, product strategy, product teams, product discovery, product delivery (S045). Four competencies companies often lack even when they have the titles: a real product manager, a real product designer, a real tech lead, and a real product leader (S001).
- **When to use:** A company wants to "become product-led," a CEO is funding projects, or a transformation is a rename. Also the fit test for traditional IT, a government, or a team that mostly buys software.
- **How to score:** Guidance: the point is outcomes, which he also frames for CEOs as time to money (S001, S045, S050). Fail the fit test if the team cannot move value, if a third-party product leaves no levers, if engineers are not in the work daily, or if a date outranks the outcome (S050). If you are not building something new with engineers, it is not for you (S050). Shipping on time without the outcome is rarely a win (S050). Both predictability and outcomes matter; score which one wins the tie (S050). Leaders who have never lived the model need coaching, not only a few new hires (S040).
- **Quote:** "These five product model concepts form the foundation of the product model." (S045)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S019, S040, S043, S044, S045, S048, S049, S050)

## 5. Focus (pick a few battles) *(unnamed as a single label; "focus" and "pick our battles" are his words)*
- **What it is:** Product strategy as the choice of a very small number of problems that would cause a cascade of good results. Prioritizing is not focusing. A fair split of engineering time across stakeholders, his Pandora example, produces activity and little innovation (S026). The 2007 version is personal: separate the important from the urgent, because most features do not matter and many make the product worse (S004).
- **Parts:** Leaders choose. The head of product does not facilitate a compromise. Work-in-progress limits matter more across the whole product organization than inside one team. Focus means stopping, postponing, or refusing the rest. Revisit at least quarterly (S045).
- **When to use:** A roadmap, an OKR list, a sales queue, or a strategy deck has more than a handful of "critical" bets. Also when a founder says yes to a big customer to keep the peace.
- **How to score:** Guidance: his line is 2 or 3 truly important things, against the 20 or 30 he usually finds (S026). If leaders will not choose, the strategy is doomed at the start (S026). A ranked list that still funds everything scores as prioritizing without focus (S026). Saying no, including shutting a big effort down, scores high (S044, S047). Strategy with no insight behind the choice is hope (inferred from S026, S027).
- **Quote:** "If success is a function of picking the right battles, product strategy is how we pick those battles." (S045)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S004, S023, S026, S044, S045, S047, S049)

## 6. Team objectives (the alternative to roadmaps)
- **What it is:** Replace the feature roadmap with context. One product vision for the company, plus a few business objectives per team. He has used OKRs for this since at least 2015 (S015). The team is told the problem and how success will be measured, then picks the solution. He borrows the military idea of commander's intent: what you want and why, not the maneuver.
- **Parts:** An objective is the problem. Two key results: a primary business measure, and a quality backstop so the team does not hit the number by harming something else (S028). The team proposes the numbers and the timeframe. Leaders assign the problems and do not assign the features. Unavoidable dates are high-integrity commitments, not roadmap rows.
- **When to use:** Planning a quarter, judging a roadmap, or a stakeholder wants a feature called "integrate PayPal" with no statement of the business result. Also when OKRs have decayed into a list of tasks.
- **How to score:** Guidance: key results are business results. Activities and deliverables as key results are the common failure (S028). The work is not done when it ships. It is done when the key result moves. His example bar: onboarding that takes 30 days must get under 3 hours (S015). If the team needs time to find the right measure on a new problem, allow it (S028). Grade last year's roadmap by how many items met the business objective (S015). A low hit rate means stop feeding a feature list.
- **Quote:** "The idea is simple enough: tell the team what you need them to accomplish, and how the results will be measured, and let the team figure out the best way to solve the problems." (S015)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S014, S015, S016, S023, S028, S045)

## 7. Customer inspired, technology enabled
- **What it is:** Where solutions come from. Customers supply the pain. They do not supply the invention, because they do not know what is now possible, do not know what they want until they see it, and do not know each other (S002, S020). He splits needs into unmet (the customer is already frustrated) and unrealized (they only see it once a solution exists) (S012). The 2006 line and the 2017 line are the same claim.
- **Parts:** Watch customers, especially in their own setting. Put at least one engineer in the room. Hand engineers constraints (legal, financial, sales, marketing, manufacturing), not a requirements document. Give them time in discovery. Judge the product team on one set of outcomes, not engineers on output and the product manager on results. The product manager has to be confident enough to ask the engineers.
- **When to use:** The plan is "we asked customers what to build," a sales team is collecting requirements, or a new technology showed up and the company is bolting it onto the old workflow. Benedict Evans's line, which he cites, is that people first fit the new tool to the old way of working, then change the work (S041). Use the second move.
- **How to score:** Guidance: a feature list taken from interviews scores low. It yields what he calls a random collection of band-aid features, and it only reaches unmet needs (S002, S012). Score high when the team has seen the pain and a technology makes a solution people did not know to request (S020, S031). Using engineers only to code captures about half their value (S020). You still need at least one engineer per team who will engage at this level. Not every engineer wants to (S020).
- **Quote:** "One of the most enduring myths in the product world is that great products result from listening to your customers (or prospective customers)." (S020)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S012, S017, S020, S027, S031, S041)

## 8. Principles over process
- **What it is:** Teach the underlying principles so a person can tell when a technique fits and can judge a new technique quickly. Techniques change. Principles last (S022). His theory of Agile is that process people took over a principle-based manifesto, and most companies now run the rituals without the principles (S039).
- **Parts:** There is no single discovery process and no single delivery process (S019). A team should be skilled in more than one, and pick with judgement. Culture and coaching beat a standard method. A model is useful and becomes harmful when it turns into a script.
- **When to use:** Someone wants to install SAFe, a canvas, a certification, a dual-track phase gate, or "the Spotify model." Also when a new technique, including generative AI, shows up and people want a rule.
- **How to score:** Guidance: if the team can name the principle and why this technique serves it, score high. If they can name the ritual and not the principle, score low (S039). He retired his own label, Dual-Track Agile, for this reason (S009). Certifications say nothing about the skills that matter (S046). His view of SAFe is second-hand: he says he knows of no strong product company that uses it, and he has not worked it himself (S016). On generative AI he says the principles are stable, the techniques are not, and he has not figured the techniques out (S001).
- **Quote:** "while the techniques change fairly constantly, the underlying principles endure." (S022)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S009, S018, S019, S022, S039, S047)

## 9. High-integrity commitments
- **What it is:** The compromise he offers stakeholders who need a date. The team gets a little discovery time first. In that time they test value and usability with customers, feasibility with engineers, and viability with stakeholders. Then they either give a date and the expected business result with high confidence, or they say the work is not worth doing (S010).
- **Parts:** Discovery before the promise. Delivery managers help set the date, because they know availability and dependencies. Engineer estimates alone are not the commitment. Keep the number of these promises small. A high-integrity business case is the same move: evidence first, then the investment, as in Spotify rebuilding its playlist system only after Discover Weekly showed demand (S044).
- **When to use:** A stakeholder, a contract, a launch, or a board wants a date. Also when a team is making promises in a roadmap meeting before anyone has a solution.
- **How to score:** Guidance: a date set before discovery is the failure mode. Those promises are made too early, and before anyone knows the solution will work (S010). Good product companies minimize commitments (S010). After discovery, a date plus an expected result scores high. A date with no result, or a refusal to allow any discovery, scores low. He still says some real commitments are required to run a company (S010). Many ideas will not work, and the ones that do take several iterations, so a commitment that assumes the first idea is the product scores low (S010, S011).
- **Quote:** "Discovery work is all about addressing these risks and answering these questions before we spend the time and money to build production-quality products." (S010)
- **Sources:** 6 (S010, S014, S015, S044, S048, S050)

## 10. Flavors of prototypes
- **What it is:** Pick the prototype that matches the question. He names four, plus hybrids: feasibility, low-fidelity user, high-fidelity user, and live-data (S013). Teams get in trouble by using one flavor to answer a question it cannot answer.
- **Parts:** Feasibility: engineers write just enough code to answer a technical risk. It can be thrown away. Low-fidelity: an interactive wireframe for workflow and early usability. It does not show visual design or real data. High-fidelity: looks real, is not live, good for finding why people do not like it and for showing stakeholders. It does not prove the product will sell. Live-data: a narrow implementation on real data and real traffic, so you can compare it with the current product. Developers have to build it. It is not yet a product you can run the business on. Hybrids: for search and recommendations, live data without live traffic, judged by talking with users.
- **When to use:** Any discovery question. Also when a team wants to show a polished prototype to ten people and call the idea proven, or when they want to build the real product in order to learn.
- **How to score:** Guidance: his user-test bar is 6 consecutive users who understand the value and can finish the key tasks (S005). Fix a big problem after 2 or 3 users. Do not wait to be hit by 8 in a row (S005). Ten or fifteen people saying they love a beautiful prototype is not proof it will sell (S013). Use willingness to pay on a 1 to 10 scale, even if you will not charge that way, and track the average as the prototype changes (S005). A live-data prototype that beats the current product on real use scores as evidence. A live-data prototype still has to be productized afterward (S013). The product manager attends every test and does not delegate it (S005).
- **Quote:** "there are in fact many very different forms of prototypes, each with different characteristics and each suited to testing different things." (S013)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S009, S013, S032, S044, S045)

## 11. Discovery judgement *(his four areas)*
- **What it is:** Product work is judgement about how much discovery a given item deserves. He names four areas: how big the risk is, how much evidence is enough, how much of the product you need to touch, and how much you need to write down (S032).
- **Parts:** For each of the four risks, judge severity and consequence. Evidence runs from a few users' observed behavior up to a statistically significant live-data test. Save the large live-data tests for risky, high-consequence bets. Make most decisions on faster qualitative tests. Projects and initiatives are risky almost by definition. Documentation should be only what the team and the stakeholders need. Prototypes describe behavior better than most documents. Do not turn the write-up into a template.
- **When to use:** Planning a discovery sprint, a founder wants statistical proof of every idea, or a team is writing the same spec for a one-line copy change and for a pricing change.
- **How to score:** Guidance: if nothing is really at risk, add it to the backlog and move on (S032). If every item gets the maximum test, the team is too slow and the true risks starve (S032). An employee trial is not a predictive test when employees are not the users (S044). You can misjudge a minor risk and learn the hard way. He still says that costs less than treating everything as major (S032). New people need a manager in the judgement. Experience is an advantage here (S032).
- **Quote:** "I like to save the large scale, live-data tests for the truly risky, high consequence issues, and I like to make the bulk of decisions on much faster, albeit lower confidence, qualitative tests." (S032)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S013, S018, S032, S042, S044)

## 12. Missionaries, not mercenaries
- **What it is:** The staffing and leadership test behind the team types. He quotes John Doerr for the line, and then uses it as his own: missionaries own the problem and the user; mercenaries build what they are told. The results, morale, and speed are not close (S016).
- **Parts:** Three reasons a company stays mercenary: leaders who want followers, staff who are mercenaries (including engineers who work for another company), and processes built for that model, SAFe being his example. The change path: teach the leaders, raise the bar on staff, move to durable cross-functional teams, and start from a vision plus measurable outcomes (S016).
- **When to use:** Outsourcing the engineers, a command-and-control roadmap, or a transformation that leaves the same incentives in place. Also when a founder asks why a talented team feels slow.
- **How to score:** Guidance: engineers under contract to build what you tell them are mercenaries, and he thinks a missionary team is nearly impossible in that setup (S016). Order-takers with no user contact score low (S014). Teams that believe the vision and can argue about the path score high (S023). Co-locate the trio when you can (S016). Newer, 2024: remote work has cost velocity and innovation, and he does not think big companies will return to mostly co-located teams (S001). The 2020 note is different in kind: time unexpectedly at home can be used for the deep product work, and that advice was not for people doing care work (S029).
- **Quote:** "It's nearly impossible to have a team of missionaries when your engineers work for another company and are under contract to build what you tell them to." (S016)
- **Sources:** 6 (S014, S015, S016, S020, S023, S025)

## 13. Direct access (two in a box is the anti-pattern)
- **What it is:** One product person has to hold the customer, the business, and the technology. Splitting that across a product manager and a product owner, or across a product manager and a product-ops go-between, is the anti-pattern he has argued since at least 2011 (S007, S035). In 2007 he said the successful product manager sees himself as the CEO of the product (S004). Newer, 2016: the closest analogy is the CEO, and nobody reports to the product manager (S017). Newer still, 2019: the CEO line does not apply to a feature-team product manager, who is mostly a project manager (S025).
- **Parts:** Three accesses, all direct: users and customers, plus the data; business stakeholders (marketing, sales, finance, legal); engineers and the designer. Cut project management, QA gaps, research recruiting, and design gaps by hiring for those jobs, or by the engineers stepping up. Do not cut them by slicing the product manager.
- **When to use:** A founder wants an associate PM, a "business PM" and a "technical PM," or a product owner beside the product manager. Also when one product manager is covering several teams.
- **How to score:** Guidance: lose any of the three accesses and value and viability are no longer owned (S035). More than about 10 engineers per product manager is too much. Split the team (S035). A product manager who covers multiple teams devolves into delivery (S035). Early-stage startups often cannot fund the extra roles. That is a reason to keep the job whole, not a reason to invent a second product person (S035). An associate for admin creates people-management the team should not have (S035).
- **Quote:** "please do not slice up the PM role such that direct access to any of these three is lost" (S035)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S007, S017, S034, S035, S036)

## 14. Product vision versus mission
- **What it is:** Mission is a slogan of purpose, such as organizing the world's information. Product vision is a persuasive picture of the future product experience, and he cannot name a higher-leverage tool for a product organization (S030). Visiontype is his word for a video-style prototype that shows the customer's life getting better.
- **Parts:** One vision for the organization, not one per team. It creates a shared aim, keeps the work on the customer, gives meaning, shows where new technology fits, informs architecture and team topology, and helps recruiting. It is not a specification and not a feature list.
- **When to use:** Teams are locally optimizing, a strategy has no picture of the destination, or someone wants a fill-in-the-blank canvas to stand in for the vision.
- **How to score:** Guidance: if teams could mistake it for a spec, it is too detailed (S030). A roadmap list is not meaningful (S030). A canvas that nobody would be moved by fails his test (S030). Feature-team companies can skip it. His line is that mercenaries need to be paid, not inspired (S030). Renaming the mission is optional. Doing the vision work is not.
- **Quote:** "I don't think I can name a higher-leverage tool for a product organization." (S030)
- **Sources:** 5 (S015, S016, S023, S030, S048)

## 15. Instrument, then iterate until the result *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Shipping is the start of the proof. Everything that goes out is instrumented so the team can see whether the outcome happened, and the team keeps changing the solution until it does (S001, S045). He has pushed analytics-from-the-start since 2006 (S002).
- **Parts:** Build, test, deploy, instrument, read the data, iterate. Marketing announcements can be batched. The product releases stay small and frequent. Do not confuse the two (S001).
- **When to use:** A launch plan, a quarterly release train, or a team that celebrates the release. Also when a founder cannot say what will be measured the day after ship.
- **How to score:** Guidance: no instrumentation, no proof, so the launch plan scores low (S001). Quarterly releases mean you cannot learn or take care of customers at the pace you need (S001). The feature is not done until the key result moves (S015). If they cannot name the metric and the event that feeds it, they are not ready to build (inferred from S001, S028, S045).
- **Quote:** "you have to make sure that everything you release is instrumented so that we can prove the outcome." (S001)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S014, S044, S045)

## 16. Minimal product, viable product, and the MVP test
- **What it is:** His attempt to stop teams using one term for three different things. A minimal product works and is usable. A viable product is one people choose to buy or use, and that can sustain a business (S008). An MVP test is the smallest experiment for one hypothesis, not a product (S006). Product discovery, in the 2011 definition, is the use of those tests to reach product market fit, and product market fit requires evidence, which he then called validated customer learning (S006). He updated the vocabulary in January 2014 so the terms would stay distinct. He was not saying other people's definitions of MVP were wrong. He was saying the ambiguity hurts.
- **Parts:** Choose to use or buy it (value). Figure out how to use it (usability). Deliver it when needed with the resources on hand (feasibility). The 2011 definition stops at those three. Newer reading, after the 2017 to 2018 viability split: a product people love and a business that works are both required, so "viable" now includes business viability (S021, S042). Spend most discovery time on the viable part, because that is where the risk is (S008).
- **How to score:** Guidance: "could they use it" is the wrong pass. The pass is that they would choose it (S008). A task completed in a usability test is not product market fit (S008). Months of work labeled an MVP, with no evidence it will sell, scores as the misuse (S018). Opinion, including the product owner's opinion, is not evidence (S006). A prototype that works is a minimal product until there is evidence someone will choose it and the business can carry it.
- **When to use:** Someone says "MVP," "we have product market fit," or "users finished the tasks." Separate the experiment, the minimal product, and the viable product before the decision.
- **Quote:** "the smallest possible product that has three critical characteristics: people choose to use it or buy it; people can figure out how to use it; and we can deliver it when we need it with the resources available" (S006)
- **Sources:** 4 (S006, S008, S013, S018)

## 17. Four sources of product strategy insight
- **What it is:** Where the focus in framework 5 is allowed to come from. He says there is no paint-by-numbers playbook. There is homework, and then an insight you are prepared to notice (S027).
- **Parts:** Quantitative: product data, funnels, retention, live-data tests. Qualitative: evaluative (did this idea work, and why) and generative (a new opportunity). Most discovery is evaluative. Generative insight is rarer and can change the company's course from a small sample. Technology: enabling tech, often found by an engineer who brings a prototype. Industry: analysts, trends, other regions. Then a fifth move: get the insight to the leader who can connect it, by the head of product saying it out loud at an all-hands. A written dump in email or Slack rarely lands.
- **When to use:** Setting or challenging a strategy, a founder has a hunch with no study behind it, or a consultancy is offering a business strategy and calling it a product strategy.
- **How to score:** Guidance: an epiphany counts only after hours in the data, the customers, the technology, and the industry (S027). A strategy that cannot point to one of the four sources scores low. Live-data skill is a lasting advantage. Get good at it early (S027). If the technology matters, the company has to learn it, not hand it to a vendor and walk away (S027). Management consultancies, in his view, mostly know business strategy, rarely product strategy, and do not stay long enough. He has mixed feelings, because the people are often strong and can be coached into product leaders (S027).
- **Quote:** "You might have an epiphany in the shower, but that's only after you've spent hours studying your data, your customers, the enabling technologies and your industry." (S027)
- **Sources:** 4 (S020, S027, S044, S047)

## 18. What market research can and cannot answer *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A 2006 sorting of the tools, and he has not taken it back. Surveys, analytics, data mining, focus groups, site visits, user profiles, usability tests, and competitive analysis can tell you who the users are, how they use the product, where they stumble, and what they want changed. They cannot tell you what product to create (S002).
- **Parts:** Use the tools to refine a product that exists. Do not use them to conceive a new one. Visit users in their own setting. There is no substitute, even though it is expensive. Learn the major user types. There is no single user. Watch actions in usability tests. Find what each competitor actually does well.
- **When to use:** A founder wants to commission research to decide the product, or a focus group is about to vote on a design. Also when a team is writing off every competitor as clueless.
- **How to score:** Guidance: he knows of no winning product that was created by market research (S002). A research report that ends in a feature list scores as a no for a new product. The same report as one input to a prototype plan scores as useful (S002). Surveys are one input. Bad questions make the results disposable (S002). Focus groups make him uneasy, because users influence each other and cannot use what does not exist yet (S002). A focus group that says "lose the touch screen," his iPhone example, is the historical miss (S020).
- **Quote:** "I know of no winning product that was created by market research." (S002)
- **Sources:** 3 (S002, S012, S020)

## 19. Time to money
- **What it is:** His framing for a CEO or CFO. Time to market is a known technique. Time to money is the harder outcome: when the business result shows up, not when the software ships (S001). In the project model the best you can honestly ask for is time to market (S043).
- **Parts:** Fund a durable team against an outcome (churn, growth, the relevant KPI), and keep funding the iterations. A project usually gets one attempt, then the team disperses, and the learning leaves with them (S043).
- **When to use:** A budget decision, a board that wants a launch date, or a plan whose last milestone is "ship."
- **How to score:** Guidance: a plan that ends at launch scores as time to market. A plan that keeps going until the KPI moves scores as time to money. He is explicit that time to market still matters, and that a date-first effort is sometimes right. He calls that the exception (S043). Most project efforts, in his account, give up on value and try to ship something (S043).
- **Quote:** "it's about time to money more than time to market." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S043, S045)

## 20. Product opportunity assessment
- **What it is:** His 2006 lightweight check before a build, meant to replace a market-requirements document that took too long, went unread, and skipped the real questions. The product manager answers ten questions and senior management makes a go or no-go (S003). Newer practice, from the discovery articles onward: a written assessment can frame a bet and is not evidence the solution works (inferred from S003, S009, S011, S013). Keep the questions. Do not let the document substitute for a test.
- **Parts:** The problem, in one crisp statement. Who it is for. Market size, sized conservatively. Alternatives. Why you are suited. Why now. Go to market. How you will measure success or make money. Factors that are critical to the solution. A go or no-go. Two legal outcomes: kill a poor bet early, or learn what a good bet requires.
- **When to use:** A new product, a new line, or a CEO who has already said "do it." Run it anyway, so you know what you are up against. Some products are pursued for strategic reasons even when the market case is weak. He treats that as legitimate, and still wants the assessment (S003).
- **How to score:** Guidance: question one is the hard one. A list of features means it is unanswered (S003). Not every opportunity has to be a billion-dollar market (S003). A clear company-level go or no-go scores high. Gut feel, or one big customer funding a special, scores low (S003). Newer bar: a polished write-up with no user evidence is a maybe, not a go (inferred from S003, S011, S013).
- **Quote:** "The hardest question to answer is usually the first, which surprises people because it sounds like the easiest." (S003)
- **Sources:** 1 (S003)
