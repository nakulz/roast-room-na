# Stories: Marty Cagan

16 stories and cases he retells, most-reused first. Built from the verified notes on the same 50 sources as the rest of this brain. The lesson sits with the source ids. Words in quotation marks are copied from those sources.

1. **Customers could not have ordered the product that won.** He keeps the same list: players before the iPod, phones before the iPhone, search before Google, movies before Netflix, music before Spotify, team chat before Slack, lodging before Airbnb. None of Google, eBay, the iPod, or MySpace came from market research. In the profiles behind those products, the users had no idea the solution was possible. Phone makers ran focus groups and were told to drop the touch screen. Lesson: known pain is not a spec. The win is a solution people could not describe, usually opened by what technology just made possible (S002, S017, S020, S031). "I know of no winning product that was created by market research. Not Google, not eBay, not the iPod, not MySpace." (S002) "In each of these examples, the users had no idea the solution they fell in love with was actually possible." (S017) "They said to lose the touch screen." (S020)

2. **Thirty customers before he was allowed to decide.** His manager would not let him take the product manager role on a new business product until he had sat with 30 customers in person, half of them outside the US. He thought he already knew the customers, and the visits showed he did not. He used those relationships for years. By 2019 the number he gives other managers is at least 15, and he says 30 is not magic and two or three is not enough. Lesson: sit with users in their own setting before you choose what to build (S001, S024). "I was not allowed to take the product manager role until I had visited 30 customers in person, 15 in the US, 15 in Europe." (S001)

3. **Amazon Prime was a series of shipping experiments, not a spreadsheet.** Growth had slowed. Advertising produced a modest lift, and the leaders bet on making shipping better instead. Over more than two years they tested offers, an engineer named Charlie Ward tied a broken promotions system to the DVD team's subscription machinery, and Jeff Bezos widened the result into a loyalty program. The first productized version took about three months on a dedicated empowered team. The business case looked weak because of the cost. Strategy (price, selection, convenience) carried it. He cites Bezos that no customer asked for Prime. Written with Richard Russell. Lesson: run real experiments on a hard problem, let an engineer's prototype change the bet, and count the change in buying behavior, not only the fee (S020, S047). "Jeff looked at the results of more than two years of ongoing experimentation, and now saw the potential as something larger, a free shipping program as part of a larger customer loyalty program." (S047) "While Prime didn't look particularly strong as a business case due to the potential costs, it was driven forward because of a belief in the product strategy" (S047)

4. **Pandora's fair split of engineering time.** An executive published a process for dividing capacity across stakeholders. Cagan read it as prioritizing without a strategy, predicted many features and little innovation, and then watched the stock go from a 2011 IPO at $16 to about $8 before the company was sold. He has used the article for years as the case of how not to do product. Lesson: a process that gives every stakeholder a share is not focus (S026, S027). "I immediately recognized the complete and utter absence of product strategy and especially focus." (S026)

5. **Netflix in 1999, stuck at about 300,000 customers.** The rental model was an online Blockbuster, and people tried it once and forgot. Kate Arnold's team tested a subscription, then in about three months built the queue, ratings, and recommendations so the company could afford the movies people actually wanted. While they were short of cash they offered to sell to Blockbuster for $50 million and were turned down. He also points at early Netflix as a company that grew from one insight about customer behavior. Lesson: the product manager's job was a business solution, not a feature list, and a rejected sale is not the lesson he draws a rule from (S017, S027).

6. **Google AdWords in 2000.** The hard part was getting permission to work on it. Sales and engineering resisted. Jane Manning met the objectors one by one, put the new ads beside the results so they would not be confused with the ads sales had already sold, and ranked them by price per impression times click-through rate. He later sets Prime next to AdWords as the product that changed the company's course. Lesson: win the constraints one stakeholder at a time, with design and engineering in the work (S017, S047).

7. **Spotify's Discover Weekly.** Leadership put the company on the lean-back listener, the person who will not hunt for music, and shut down a large video effort to staff that problem. A hack-week add-on to Year In Music, Play It Forward, stayed in use for months and seeded the playlist. Daniel Ek was openly skeptical and still gave the team the problem. Employees were not a fair test. A rollout to 1.5 percent of users, about a million people, drew more than 1,500 responses, with more than 85 percent scoring it 4 or 5 and 65 percent finding a new favorite song. The public launch did a billion streams in ten weeks, and 71 percent of listeners added a song. Lesson: focus is a killed project, and a small live test can justify rebuilding the system underneath (S044). "Spotify's employees were not a predictive test, especially for the lean-back case." (S044) "But to his credit, he gave the team a problem to solve, and then allowed that work to proceed." (S044)

8. **The HP profiler.** At an applied research lab he paired with a senior engineer who refused a broad performance cleanup until a profiler showed the few spots where the time actually went. The engineer's line was that they could, but no. Most of the work would not even have been noticed. Lesson: measure the hot spots and ignore the rest, in the product and in the business (S026). "we could, but no." (S026)

9. **Adobe Creative Cloud, as he told it in 2016.** Lea Hickman had to move a desktop license business, on the order of $2 billion and about half of Adobe's revenue, to a subscription that worked across the devices designers were actually using. Finance was afraid of the revenue dip. She and the CTO built prototypes of the new foundation and she kept communicating until there was no such thing as too much of it. The subscription later passed $1 billion in recurring revenue faster than anyone else, and he credits the move with a much higher market value. Lesson: a big painful change needs a picture of the future and constant evidence, not a single announcement (S017).

10. **Alexa started as a speaker, not a platform plan.** An engineer prototyped the Echo as the voice controller for a streaming TV device, and that prototype let the team see a smart-home hub. Lesson: the breakthrough often starts as an engineer's small prototype, not as a grand strategy (S020).

11. **Google Translate, a decade of small gains and then one year.** The team had worked on quality for nearly ten years with little the user could feel. Machine learning produced more improvement in a year than the decade before, and they shipped it without announcing it to see whether people noticed. People did. Lesson: an enabling technology can change the product overnight, and behavior is the test (S020).

12. **Disney's MagicBand.** By 2011 the parks were crowded enough that people were less likely to come back. Disney stood up a discovery lab behind Epcot, prototyped an RFID band, and spent on the order of $1 billion on the infrastructure behind it. Guests never asked for a band. Designers and engineers saw the frustration. Lesson: a large old company still has to bet, and the bet comes from the pain plus the technology, not from a request (S020). "Customers didn't request a MagicBand in any sense, but rather the designers and engineers saw the customer frustrations, and leveraged technology to reimagine and reinvent the guest experience." (S020) "in addition to leveraging technology, large companies also need the leadership and courage to make some big bets." (S020)

13. **Workiva and the finance staff nobody thanks.** The founders met overworked accounting teams at public companies and bet early on cloud infrastructure and a rich web front end. He cites an NPS above Apple's, which he calls unheard of in enterprise software. Lesson: you do not need to be Google to work this way, and the customer can be a neglected user inside a big company (S020).

14. **The first visit to Walmart headquarters.** He was building developer tools on the assumption that other companies built the way Silicon Valley did. Walmart's equipment and way of working were a shock, and that visit became the reason he started the Silicon Valley Product Group. Lesson: the methods he teaches were invisible to him while he only worked inside the bubble, and most companies do not start there (S001). "I'm living in a bubble. Silicon Valley is not like most of the world here." (S001)

15. **Airbnb's hard part was the law, not the signup.** Getting people to sign up was easy. Making a listing legal in San Francisco was the real product risk. Lesson: viability, including compliance, is where the risk hides after the demo looks popular (S001). "Remember Airbnb, it wasn't so hard to get people to sign up. It was hard to make listings legal in San Francisco." (S001)

16. **Prototype tests in Berlin, in a language he does not speak.** He could tell which ideas were working from body language and tone. Lesson: be in the room, watch what people do, and do not wait for a lab or a perfect transcript (S005). "Recently I attended some prototype testing with one of my clients in Berlin, and even though I don't speak German, it was obvious what the issues were, and which ideas worked well and which ones didn't." (S005)
