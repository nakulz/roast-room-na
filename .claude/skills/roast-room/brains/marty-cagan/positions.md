# Positions: Marty Cagan

## Pricing
- **Stance:** No recorded position on how a product company should set a price, when to raise it, or whether to use freemium or usage-based pricing. What he does record is a discovery probe, a viability test, and a separate rule for pricing his own kind of coaching practice.
- **Discovery probe (2007-10-01):** ask what someone would pay, even when that is not the business model. "One technique I like for gauging value is to ask how much the user would be willing to pay for it, even if you have no intention of actually charging for use this way." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- **Viability (2017-12-04):** monetization and the cost to acquire sit inside business viability, which the product manager owns. "whether we can afford to cost-effectively acquire customers, whether we can effectively monetize the product, and whether the product is consistent with the brand promise" (S021, 2017-12-04)
- **Coaching practice (2023-01-11):** price the package, never the hour. "For most new product coaches, I encourage them to focus on getting somewhere between 4-7 clients that sign up for ongoing services, and pay a quarterly subscription price (of usually somewhere between USD $10K and $25K per client per quarter)." (S038, 2023-01-11) "Many new coaches make the mistake of charging an hourly rate, which is really the worst of all options." (S038, 2023-01-11) "you want the price to be for the package (the outcome), and not on a per-hour or per-day basis (the time)." (S038, 2023-01-11) Consistent revenue, for most people, takes on the order of 2 to 3 years. "realistically, for most people, it's on the order of 2-3 years before the revenue is consistent" (S038, 2023-01-11)

## First hire
- **Stance:** No recorded position on the company's first employee in general. On the first product manager, the founder keeps value and viability until the team is past a small engineering group. He looks inside the company before he looks outside, and he does not treat an MBA as product training.
- **Newer (2024-03-10):** "I'm one of the people that tries to discourage them from hiring product managers too soon because a lot of them make the mistake of hiring them too soon." (S001, 2024-03-10) "But if they're a real product manager and they're worried about value and viability, that is the founder's job." (S001, 2024-03-10) "At a certain number of engineers, usually 20 to 25, it's a lot better if the co-founder is the product person for that." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- **Early-stage staffing (2022-01-31):** "In many early stage startups, there literally are not enough funds to have people dedicated for many of the roles" (S035, 2022-01-31)

(S004, 2007-09-10) "a problem with MBA programs, even from the top-tier schools, is that they almost never teach product management" (S004, 2007-09-10) "But few hires you make will be as critical as your product managers, so it is worthwhile to interview for these characteristics and set the bar high." (S004, 2007-09-10) "if your goal is a truly good product, it is simply not going to happen if you can't find a truly bright product manager." (S004, 2007-09-10)

## Firing
- **Stance:** No recorded position on severance, a performance plan, or how to run a layoff. He does say when to move someone out of the product role, what to cut first when cost is the issue, and that a leader who will not change can end a transformation.
- **Move them out of the role (2019-04-22):** "If they are not good at these skills, and they show no sincere interest in improving, then that's when the manager needs to help the person find a more suitable job." (S024, 2019-04-22)
- **What to cut (2024-01-30):** a feature-team product manager is a project manager with the wrong title. "And if you are a CEO that is looking to cut costs, wouldn't you rather cut this, than lose a hands-on designer or engineer?" (S046, 2024-01-30) Some layoffs cannot be avoided (S046).
- **The call companies dodge (2024-06-25):** "Maybe a CTO is simply not willing to change." (S049, 2024-06-25) He treats that kind of call as what makes or breaks a move to the product model (S049).

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Leaders pick two or three problems that matter and say no to the rest. A fair split of engineering time across stakeholders is prioritizing without focus, and it is how strategy dies. Pleasing every customer type in one product pleases no one.
- "If the leaders are not willing or able to make these choices, then the product strategy is doomed from the start." (S026, 2020-02-18)
- "The bottom line is that organization will get more critical work accomplished if it focuses on just a few at a time." (S026, 2020-02-18)
- "Instead of 2-3 truly important things, they have at least 20-30." (S026, 2020-02-18)
- "If success is a function of picking the right battles, product strategy is how we pick those battles." (S045, 2024-01-17)
- "This focus meant saying no to many other potential opportunities, and postponing or discontinuing others." (S044, 2023-10-18)
- "they too often try to please too many types of customers in a single product and they end up pleasing no one." (S023, 2018-10-31)
- "This is why the strategy of being everything to everyone rarely works." (S038, 2023-01-11)
- "Maybe the company is trying to do too many things at once, and it needs to focus." (S049, 2024-06-25)
- He cites Pandora's stakeholder-capacity process as the case of no strategy at all. "I immediately recognized the complete and utter absence of product strategy and especially focus." (S026, 2020-02-18)

## Niche
- **Stance:** The market does not have to be huge, and the estimate should be conservative. The failure mode he names is a product, or a coaching offer, aimed at everyone.

- For a coaching business he makes the product analogy explicit. "However, just as it's very difficult to create a product that tries to please everyone, it's very difficult to create a product coaching business that tries to please everyone." (S038, 2023-01-11) "focus on one type of product coaching for one type of client." (S038, 2023-01-11)

## AI
- **Stance:** Strong product companies should run at a new technology by prototyping with the teams, and they should not pretend they can predict the impact. Ethics will get skipped in a race to market. His how-to for using the tools changed in 2024. He says he does not have the techniques figured out.
- **2023-03-21:** "In strong product companies, we live for these disruptive technologies." (S041, 2023-03-21) "how can we use this new technology to solve problems for our customers, that we have never been able to solve before?" (S041, 2023-03-21) "While I hesitate to make predictions on something so disruptive so soon, I do believe that the coming years will shine a very strong light on product management." (S041, 2023-03-21) "Sadly our industry does not have a good track record in terms of paying attention to the ethical risks." (S041, 2023-03-21) "I am also concerned that the race to market will ignore many of the unintended consequences to people and society." (S041, 2023-03-21)
- **He dropped the rush to predict (2023-03-21):** "I used to stress about figuring out the impact of new technologies as fast as possible because I felt the clock ticking." (S041, 2023-03-21) "But now I've learned that it's really not possible to truly predict the impact of disruptive technologies." (S041, 2023-03-21)
- **Newer, how to use the model (2024-03-10):** he used to say start with ChatGPT. He reversed that. "I don't have it figured out. In fact, I recently changed my advice" (S001, 2024-03-10) "Now I've been recommending to people that they think through the answer first." (S001, 2024-03-10) "If you are fundamentally a backlog administrator, good luck protecting that" (S001, 2024-03-10) "viability becomes even more the important question." (S001, 2024-03-10) Impact on the work shows up in engineering first, then design and product management (S041, S001).

## Remote work
- **Stance:** He wants the product trio co-located when he can get it, because discovery and speed suffer apart. The newer view adds that the damage is real and that big companies are not going back.
- **2007-10-01:** remote prototype tests are extra testing, not a replacement. "to me this is not a substitute for face-to-face testing." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- **2015-11-24:** "If the company isn't set up this way already, then it's critical to move aggressively to durable, cross-functional, co-located (whenever possible) product teams" (S016, 2015-11-24)
- **2020-09-10:** "One of the side benefits of co-located product teams is that the engineers are so involved in the discovery work that when something is ready to be built, they often know precisely what they need to do." (S032, 2020-09-10) When the team is apart, engineers ask for more documentation (S032).
- **2020-03-12, pandemic note:** he treats freed time at home as a chance for the deep product work managers say they cannot get to, and he sets the list aside for people carrying care duties (S029). That is not a claim that a remote team innovates as well.
- **Newer (2024-03-10):** "with remote employees, both velocity and innovation have taken a real hit." (S001, 2024-03-10) "I don't think we're ever going to go back to the days of big companies having almost all co-located teams." (S001, 2024-03-10)

## Work hours
- **Stance:** The product manager job is heavy, and a person who needs to be told to show up at a critical moment is the wrong hire. Speed still comes from technique, not from longer weeks. The 70-hour warning is under Burnout.
- "However, the product manager role is not for someone who is afraid of hard work." (S004, 2007-09-10)
- "if you have to actually ask or tell the product manager to come in during a critical point you have the wrong person for the job." (S004, 2007-09-10)
- "Good teams understand the need for speed and how rapid iteration is the key to innovation, and they understand this speed comes from the right techniques and not forced labor." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "It is absolutely essential to get very skilled at distinguishing that which is important from that which is urgent, and learn to prioritize and plan your time." (S004, 2007-09-10)

## Burnout
- **Stance:** A 70-hour week is evidence the person is chasing urgent work and missing the job, not evidence of commitment.
- "I have known too many product managers that burn themselves out with 70-hour weeks and the worst part is when I tell them that they're not actually doing their job." (S004, 2007-09-10)
- He has felt the pressure himself. "I've never felt more pressure than during my early stage startup experiences" (S035, 2022-01-31)

## Culture
- **Stance:** He wants missionaries who own outcomes, innovation ahead of predictability, and competence with the toxic people filtered out. The word culture, used as a slogan, is too vague for him, because the strong companies do not share one culture. Copying radical transparency is a special case, not the default.
- He uses John Doerr's line. "we need teams of missionaries, not teams of mercenaries." (S016, 2015-11-24)
- "Good teams have a compelling product vision that they pursue with a missionary-like passion." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "innovation is more important than predictability." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "yet their culture and behaviors consistently prioritize predictability over results." (S043, 2023-08-17)
- "It's much more about putting in place the necessary culture, and training your team on the critical techniques." (S019, 2017-08-07)
- **Cultural fit (2018-10-31):** he calls the hiring screen harmful. "This is probably one of the most damaging concepts to your efforts to build a great organization." (S023, 2018-10-31) "just filter out the relatively few assholes." (S023, 2018-10-31) "Amazon, Google, Apple and Netflix are all very strong product companies, yet they each have very different cultures." (S023, 2018-10-31) "Don't get me wrong, I still believe culture is extremely important" (S023, 2018-10-31)
- **Radical transparency (2017-12-27):** fascinating, and a poor fit for most tech companies. "I think an early stage startup could successfully adopt this culture, if the co-founders were all-in" (S022, 2017-12-27) "It's just that this culture is so extreme that it requires a very specific cultural fit." (S022, 2017-12-27) He says converting an existing company would be extremely hard unless it turned over probably 50 to 80 percent of the employees, especially the managers (S022).
- **10x people (2018-10-31):** they exist, and a toxic one does net harm. "if that 10X employee brings along toxic behaviors, you will likely cause far more damage than good." (S023, 2018-10-31) Strong companies get more from ordinary competent people than from a hunt for magic hires (S023).

## Metrics
- **Stance:** Instrument every release and judge the team on a business result, not on the act of shipping. Key results are outcomes, with a second number that stops you from cheating the first. Know the finance ratio of lifetime value to the cost to acquire.
- "you have to make sure that everything you release is instrumented so that we can prove the outcome." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "Good teams instrument their work so that they can immediately understand how their product is being used and make adjustments based on the data." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "the product team measures itself against business results and not just shipping features" (S019, 2017-08-07)
- "The second most common reason that teams go wrong with OKR's is that they end up listing activities or deliverables as their key results." (S028, 2020-03-02) "it is very easy to ship a deliverable, yet not solve the underlying problem." (S028, 2020-03-02)
- "Generally speaking, we want two key results for each objective." (S028, 2020-03-02) One is the primary measure, one is a quality backstop (S028).
- "Make sure you have a clear and deep understanding of the various KPIs that describe your product's health." (S029, 2020-03-12) He wants the product manager to know whether lifetime value is enough relative to the cost to acquire (S024, 2019-04-22).
- Evidence of product market fit is not an opinion. "I define product discovery as the process of how we utilize MVP Tests to hopefully achieve Product Market Fit." (S006, 2011-08-24)

## Competition
- **Stance:** Do not write competitors off, and do not obsess over them. Learn what each does well. Obsess over reference customers. If the other teams are good, you need every prototype flavor.
- "too frequently product teams write off competitors as clueless, but in my experience every product has at least some things that the product does well" (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "Good teams obsess over their reference customers." (S014, 2014-06-13) "Bad teams obsess over competitors." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "if you are competing against good product teams, you are going to want to be skilled at developing and testing with each of these." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- A new product manager should write a narrative on the top three to five players (S024, 2019-04-22). The 2006 opportunity assessment asks for the alternatives and why you would win (S003).

## Timing
- **Stance:** Ask why this is possible now. Once a team starts on a problem, management's clock is already running. He knows he is early on when change will land, and he no longer tries to call the impact of a new technology on a deadline.
- The 2006 assessment includes a why-now market window among the ten questions (S003, 2006-12-13).
- "as soon as you start working on a problem, as far as your management is concerned, the clock starts ticking." (S031, 2020-09-04)
- Strategy gets revisited on a short cycle. "the product leaders are constantly monitoring the learnings and insights, and adjusting course no less than quarterly." (S045, 2024-01-17)
- **His own bias (2024-03-10):** "I think things are going to happen sooner than they really do." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- **Newer on prediction (2023-03-21):** "But now I've learned that it's really not possible to truly predict the impact of disruptive technologies." (S041, 2023-03-21)
- On Amazon, with Richard Russell, a long horizon is not an excuse to move slowly. "Amazon moves faster than most startups." (S047, 2024-03-26) "Often one or two quarters, and what's especially remarkable is how few of those short-term efforts generate even modest returns." (S047, 2024-03-26)

## Product discovery
- **Stance:** This is the core skill. At least half of ideas will not work, and the ones that work take several iterations. Validate in the fastest, cheapest way, with the risks tackled before production code. A weak solution can look like weak demand. Most of the time should go to the solution, after a quick check that the problem is real.
- **Earlier (2006-12-13):** a lightweight ten-question opportunity assessment, ending in a go or no-go, so bad ideas die before the build. "too often the process of deciding whether or not to build a product is left to intuition" (S003, 2006-12-13) "It is just as important that bad product ideas get identified at this stage, before significant time and cost is lost chasing them." (S003, 2006-12-13) "The hardest question to answer is usually the first, which surprises people because it sounds like the easiest." (S003, 2006-12-13) A feature list means question one is unanswered (S003).
- **Newer, and the one he repeats:** evidence and prototypes, not a document and not a launch. "The first such truth is that at least half of our ideas are just not going to work." (S011, 2013-03-17) "This is why I view product discovery as the most important core competency of a product organization." (S011, 2013-03-17) "If we can prototype and validate ideas with customers, engineers and stakeholders in days rather than months, it changes both the dynamics and the results." (S011, 2013-03-17) "I promise you that behind every successful product there are many iterations and prototypes." (S011, 2013-03-17)
- "Actually building and launching a product idea is generally the slowest, most expensive way to validate the idea." (S009, 2012-09-17) "Remember that our higher order objective is to validate our ideas the fastest, cheapest way possible." (S009, 2012-09-17)
- "Product discovery is about rapidly experimenting, mostly using prototypes, to try to come up with a solution that solves for all of the constraints and achieves the necessary outcome." (S045, 2024-01-17)
- "Few things are more wasteful and frustrating than spending a ton of energy solving a problem that customers don't care about." (S031, 2020-09-04) "The vast majority of product efforts fail not because of demand, but because they can't come up with a solution good enough to get people to switch." (S031, 2020-09-04) "if you have a crappy solution, it can look to you like the demand isn't there." (S031, 2020-09-04)
- "Ultimately our job in product discovery is not just to validate the problem, but to come up with a solution that customers love, yet works for our business." (S031, 2020-09-04)
- Would they choose it, not could they operate it. "there are countless products that would work if people choose to use them, but the problem is that people don't choose to use them." (S008, 2012-03-07)
- Size the test to the risk. "if you were to treat every risk as a major risk, then a) you'll move way too slowly; and b) you probably won't have the time you need for the items that truly are major risks." (S032, 2020-09-10) "I like to save the large scale, live-data tests for the truly risky, high consequence issues, and I like to make the bulk of decisions on much faster, albeit lower confidence, qualitative tests." (S032, 2020-09-10)
- "One of the most enduring myths in the product world is that great products result from listening to your customers (or prospective customers)." (S020, 2017-09-25)
- A strong founder can still win with rough technique. "it almost doesn't matter what techniques they use." (S001, 2024-03-10)

## Roadmaps
- **Stance:** A roadmap of features and dates is the old command model, and it is where the waste is. Replace it with a product vision, a few business objectives per team, and high-integrity commitments only where a date is truly required.
- "Every week I continue to find product teams laboring away on old-style product roadmaps that have been painstakingly negotiated with management and stakeholders, sometimes for several quarters in advance." (S011, 2013-03-17)
- "In several articles I have highlighted the reasons I see typical product roadmaps as the source of so much waste in product teams." (S015, 2015-09-07)
- "Roadmaps derive from the old centralized command-and-control model." (S015, 2015-09-07)
- "The idea is simple enough: tell the team what you need them to accomplish, and how the results will be measured, and let the team figure out the best way to solve the problems." (S015, 2015-09-07)
- "A list of features on a roadmap is not meaningful." (S030, 2020-08-04)
- "Bad teams hold meetings to generate prioritized roadmaps." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "It is all about outcome rather than output." (S015, 2015-09-07)
- Grade last year's roadmap by how many items met the business objective, not by how many shipped (S015).

## Outcomes versus output
- **Stance:** Shipping is the easy part. The team is done when the business result shows up, and the funding model has to match that or the company will keep buying dates.
- "It is a lot easier to deliver output than it is to deliver outcomes." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "The measure is it solves the problem." (S001, 2024-03-10) "You just don't get points for shipping, you get points for delivering the value." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "Today, this is commonly referred to as moving from output to outcomes." (S045, 2024-01-17) "I also like to explain this as moving from time-to-market to time-to-money." (S045, 2024-01-17) "it's about time to money more than time to market." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "Learning to consistently achieve outcomes is unquestionably tougher than delivering outputs, but this is, at its core, what the product model is all about, and what SVPG is all about sharing." (S045, 2024-01-17)
- "What is hard is embracing the many changes required to adopt a culture of outcomes." (S045, 2024-01-17)
- "In the project model, the best you can really ask for is time-to-market." (S043, 2023-08-17) "The result is that any form of innovation is exceptionally rare with projects." (S043, 2023-08-17)
- He has been on this since 2007. "I started writing about the importance of focusing on achieving outcomes over just shipping output back in 2007" (S045, 2024-01-17)
- Good teams celebrate the outcome. "Bad teams celebrate when they finally release something (output)." (S014, 2014-06-13)

## Feature teams versus empowered teams
- **Stance:** A feature team is handed a roadmap and measured on shipping it. An empowered team is handed one or two hard problems a quarter and measured on whether the problem is solved. Leaders place the bets. Teams choose the solution. Empowerment is not the team picking its own strategy.
- "instead of being given that roadmap of features, they're given problems to solve." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "Empowerment does not mean you set up this product team and they go decide what to work on." (S001, 2024-03-10) "empowerment means the leaders do their job, come up with the bets, and then the teams are able to figure out the best way to solve those problems." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "The litmus test for empowerment is that the team is able to decide the best way to solve the problems they have been assigned" (S023, 2018-10-31)
- "an empowered product team can do everything a feature team can do and more." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "The result is that in feature teams, the product manager role is mainly project manager, and partly (unskilled) designer." (S025, 2019-08-29)
- "These feature teams will often think they are doing product discovery, but really it's just design and maybe a little usability testing." (S025, 2019-08-29)
- "I have never tried to hide my strong bias toward truly empowered product teams." (S025, 2019-08-29)
- **Limit he states (2019-08-29):** "However, I will admit that even in what I consider the best product companies, not every product team is empowered." (S025, 2019-08-29) Some stay feature teams until trust is earned, or because a leader wants to dictate the solution (S025).
- "product teams don't do product strategy. Product leaders do product strategy." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- Quarterly releases cannot learn. "You cannot take care of your customers. You cannot learn at the pace you need to." (S001, 2024-03-10) Batch the marketing announcement, and let the teams ship small and often (S001).
- "the better companies do way more with a lot less." (S001, 2024-03-10)

## Prototypes
- **Stance:** A prototype is the spec and the test. Pick the flavor that matches the question. A pretty prototype that ten people say they love is not proof. Watch behavior, and the product manager has to be in the room.
- "testing your ideas with real users is probably the SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT ACTIVITY in your job as product manager." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- "I view the high-fidelity prototype as the primary means of describing the product to be built." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- "So watch what they do more than what they say." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- "Of all the different MVP techniques, prototypes are among my favorites." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "the key principle in product discovery is to come up with the fastest, cheapest way to test out your ideas." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "People say all kinds of things and then go do something different." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "Where a lot of people go sideways is when they create a beautiful user prototype, and put it in front of 10 or 15 people that say they love it, and then they declare victory." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "my favorite use of a high-fidelity prototype is not to see if users like it, but rather, to try to learn why they don't." (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "The main purpose of a live-data prototype is to actually prove something" (S013, 2014-02-23)
- "you need to make sure you are at every single test. Do not delegate this." (S005, 2007-10-01)
- He does not need a lab. "I can't count how many prototypes I've tested at a tiny table at Starbucks" (S005, 2007-10-01)
- Shelving a failed idea saves the build and the opportunity cost of the engineers (S005).

## OKRs
- **Stance:** An objective is the problem. Key results are business results the team proposes, not a list of tasks. He has preferred this since 2015, and he thinks most companies use the name for something else.
- **2015-09-07:** "There are several systems and methodologies for managing these business objectives, but my current favorite is the OKR system" (S015, 2015-09-07) It takes a few quarters to pay off (S015).
- **Still the mechanism in 2018:** "This is where empowerment becomes real, and not just a buzzword." (S023, 2018-10-31) The team gets a few problems and proposes the measures (S023).
- **2020-03-02, the failure mode:** "There is so much widespread confusion out there on OKR's, it's a very difficult topic to discuss because everyone brings their own very different experiences and perspective to the conversation." (S028, 2020-03-02) "So the actual quantitative values will come from the teams." (S028, 2020-03-02) Do not pick a measure only because it is easy to count (S028).
- If OKRs are already producing consistent innovation, he tells that company to skip his series (S028).

## Stakeholders and commitments
- **Stance:** Stakeholders hold real constraints (sales, marketing, finance, legal, brand). They do not get to hand the team a solution. A date is allowed after discovery, and good companies keep those dates rare. A sales-driven roadmap is why so much B2B software is bad. A room full of stakeholders is design by committee.
- "now you've reverted to design by committee." (S001, 2024-03-10) "this is design by committee and it rarely yields anything beyond mediocrity." (S017, 2016-09-30)
- "Bad teams gather requirements from sales and customers." (S014, 2014-06-13) "Bad teams complain about being a sales-driven company." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "this is not an accident why most B2B software is such crap." (S001, 2024-03-10) He notes that a sales-driven company can still be very valuable, and he still does not want that product (S001).
- "Good teams make high-integrity commitments after they've evaluated the request and ensured they have a viable solution that will actually work for the customer and the business." (S014, 2014-06-13)
- "Good product companies minimize these commitments." (S010, 2012-12-15) "They are made too early. They are made before we know if we can actually deliver on this obligation" (S010, 2012-12-15) "Discovery work is all about addressing these risks and answering these questions before we spend the time and money to build production-quality products." (S010, 2012-12-15)
- "The product team asks for a little time to do product discovery before commitments are made" (S010, 2012-12-15)
- "in a product company, this won't fly." (S010, 2012-12-15) Iterating until the business says it is satisfied is a custom-software habit (S010).
- "most enterprise software companies are terrible examples of best practices, and they were literally building what their customers were asking for." (S020, 2017-09-25)
- Preview a risky solution with the stakeholder before the team builds it (S024).

## Vision
- **Stance:** One persuasive picture of the future product, for the whole organization. A slogan, a canvas, or a feature list is not a vision. Feature-team companies can skip it, because mercenaries only need to be paid.
- "I don't think I can name a higher-leverage tool for a product organization." (S030, 2020-08-04)
- "Have you ever been inspired by a canvas or board?" (S030, 2020-08-04)
- "so many people fall for the trap of thinking there's a simple fill-in-the-blanks framework, canvas or board that easily gives them what they need." (S030, 2020-08-04)
- "don't need to be inspired, just paid." (S030, 2020-08-04)
- Keep it from turning into a specification, or teams will build the vision instead of solving this quarter's problem (S030). One vision for the organization, not one per team (S030).

## Strategy
- **Stance:** Strategy is the focus decision, built from homework on data, customers, technology, and the industry. There is no canvas that produces it. Leaders who will not choose have no strategy. Insights can come from anyone, and only if the person was prepared to see them.
- "if you're looking for some paint-by-number playbook or framework for coming up with these insights and a solid product strategy, I'll save you some time and tell you right now you're not going to find that here." (S027, 2020-02-19)
- "You might have an epiphany in the shower, but that's only after you've spent hours studying your data, your customers, the enabling technologies and your industry." (S027, 2020-02-19)
- "qualitative insights are often profound, and can literally change the course of your company" (S027, 2020-02-19)
- "In the best organizations, it is the engineers that often identify these enabling technologies and proactively bring the possibilities to the leaders, usually in the form of a prototype." (S027, 2020-02-19)
- "I also pointed out how most teams don't actually have a product strategy." (S029, 2020-03-12)
- Management consultancies, in his experience, do business strategy and rarely know the difference (S027). He has mixed feelings: the people are often strong and can be coached into product leaders (S027).
- He quotes Rumelt that good strategy puts energy on one or a very few pivotal objectives (S026). That line is Rumelt's.

## Product operating model
- **Stance:** The model is how you decide which problems to solve, how you discover a solution, and how you deliver. It is a set of principles shared by companies that keep innovating, not a process and not a Silicon Valley costume. It does not fit a team that cannot affect the outcome. Most transformations fail, usually because the leaders have never worked this way.
- "it boils down to a set of 20 principles and those 20 principles are what we find." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "These five product model concepts form the foundation of the product model." (S045, 2024-01-17) The five are culture, strategy, teams, discovery, and delivery (S045).
- "We were simply sharing the principles that we found in the leading product organizations." (S050, 2024-07-09)
- "If you're not working with a team of engineers to build something new, it's not for you." (S050, 2024-07-09)
- "the highest order objective of the product model is to deliver outcomes" (S050, 2024-07-09)
- "project-based funding models are designed for predictability, but undermine outcomes." (S050, 2024-07-09)
- "predictability is also important to product organizations, but for them, shipping something on time that does not deliver the necessary outcome is rarely helpful." (S050, 2024-07-09)
- "It's no secret that the overwhelming majority of companies globally that have attempted to transform have failed." (S040, 2023-02-20) Money is not the reason (S040). The usual gap is leaders who have never experienced the model, so hiring a few experienced leaders plus coaching is the path he names, and hiring a few leaders is not enough bandwidth (S040).
- "product managers don't create products; product teams do." (S049, 2024-06-25) Training only the product managers changes little if design and engineering do not change with them (S049).
- He will not try to convert a company that wants to stay a feature team. "I don't try to convince them otherwise." (S046, 2024-01-30)
- Test the new way with a pilot team, not a company-wide switch (S048, 2024-05-14).

## Process
- **Stance:** Principles last and techniques change. Agile and Lean values stay. The rituals, the certifications, and SAFe do not, and he says his view of SAFe is second-hand. He also retired his own process name when it made people stare at the process.
- "The principles are more important than process." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "I have found over the years that while the techniques change fairly constantly, the underlying principles endure." (S022, 2017-12-27)
- "My theory is that process people essentially took over Agile, and today most companies follow the processes, but aren't even close to living the principles." (S039, 2023-01-16)
- "the way Agile is actually practiced in most product companies is hardly Agile in any meaningful sense." (S018, 2017-01-29)
- "I am convinced that both Lean Startup and Agile values and principles are actually here to stay." (S018, 2017-01-29)
- "I literally don't know of a single strong product company that uses it." (S016, 2015-11-24) "what I know of SAFe is all second-hand" (S016, 2015-11-24)
- "these certifications, which in my opinion are bogus" (S001, 2024-03-10) "we remain philosophically against the many so-called certifications in the product management and product owner industry." (S046, 2024-01-30)
- "Product manager and product owner makes no sense. Huge anti-pattern." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- **Naming change:** inside the 2012 article he had already dropped the name Discovery Sprints, because it sounded like a time box. "I used to refer to this as simply Discovery Sprints, but that has an implication of time-boxed Discovery, and also serialization of phases." (S009, 2012-09-17) **Newer, update on the same page after INSPIRED V2:** "Starting with the publication of INSPIRED V2, I stopped using the term Dual-track Agile because the phrase made people focus far too much on process, and not enough on the principles." (S009, 2012-09-17) He now says continuous discovery and continuous delivery (S009). The work itself stayed. "The Discovery track is all about quickly generating validated product backlog items, and the Delivery track is all about generating releasable software." (S009, 2012-09-17)
- "All models are wrong, but some are useful." (S019, 2017-08-07) He keeps the model simple on purpose so it does not harden into a process (S019).
- "there is no single product discovery process just as there is no single product development/delivery process." (S019, 2017-08-07)

## Market research
- **Stance:** Research tells you who the users are, how they struggle, and what they want changed. It does not tell you what to build. Surveys and focus groups reach problems people already know they have. Winning products also need what is only now possible.
- "I know of no winning product that was created by market research." (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "you're in trouble if you try to steer your product with market research." (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "Winning products come from the deep understanding of the user's needs combined with an equally deep understanding of what's just now possible." (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "customers don't know what they want" (S002, 2006-06-01) They also do not know what is possible, or what the other customers need (S002).
- "I have very mixed feelings about focus groups." (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "There is no real substitute for visiting with your users in their native habitat" (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "this data is but one input to the answer" (S002, 2006-06-01)
- "customer surveys and typical market research essentially limit us to addressing unmet needs, as perceived by the customer." (S012, 2013-10-20)
- "They said to lose the touch screen." (S020, 2017-09-25) That is his exhibit for what focus groups did to the phone makers while Apple built the iPhone (S020).

## Product manager role
- **Stance:** On an empowered team the product manager is accountable for value and viability, creates the solution with design and engineering, and has nobody reporting to them. The 2007 line, that the product manager is the CEO of the product, survived with that caveat, and in 2019 he said it does not describe a feature-team product manager at all. One product manager past about ten engineers is too many.
- **2007-09-10:** "The successful product manager sees himself as the CEO of the product." (S004, 2007-09-10)
- **2016-09-30, the caveat:** "The role I would argue that the product manager is most similar to is the role of the CEO." (S017, 2016-09-30) "But with the obvious difference that unlike the CEO, nobody reports to the product manager." (S017, 2016-09-30) "The title is not important; the work they do is." (S017, 2016-09-30) "The Product Manager needs to ensure a business outcome, not just ensure a product gets defined." (S017, 2016-09-30) "don't think this means an MBA is required" (S017, 2016-09-30) "Being a great product manager means having extraordinary grit." (S017, 2016-09-30)
- **Newer scope (2019-08-29):** the CEO line is about an empowered-team product manager. "I am most definitely not talking about a product manager for a feature team." (S025, 2019-08-29)
- "product manager is a creator, not a facilitator." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "value means for the customer, viability means for your business." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "When the product succeeds, it's because everyone on the product team did what they needed to do; but when the product fails, it's the product manager." (S036, 2022-02-07) He treats this as an adage, possibly Ben Horowitz's, and he keeps it because someone has to be accountable (S036).
- "please do not slice up the PM role such that direct access to any of these three is lost" (S035, 2022-01-31) The three are customers, stakeholders, and the engineers and designer (S035).
- "If you have a single product manager responsible for providing valuable and viable solutions for more than about 10 engineers, then this may simply be too much for a single product manager to keep up with, and it may be time to split teams." (S035, 2022-01-31)
- "When you understand why engineers are so consistently the source of true innovation, you understand that if you lose this direct access, you quickly lose your chance at innovation." (S035, 2022-01-31)
- "If you're just using your engineers to code, you're only getting about half their value." (S020, 2017-09-25)
- "Giving engineers some time in discovery is easily one of the best ROI things you can do." (S020, 2017-09-25)
- Onboarding still means customers in person. His own bar was 30. His coaching bar is lower and still large. "I typically recommend at least 15 customer visits as part of new PM onboarding." (S024, 2019-04-22) "I was not allowed to take the product manager role until I had visited 30 customers in person, 15 in the US, 15 in Europe." (S001, 2024-03-10) Inferred from S001, S024: 30 was the rule that formed him, and 15 is what he tells managers now. Neither number is two or three.
- A new product manager's knowledge ramp is two to three months of several hours a day (S024). Data analysts are not there to be delegated to (S024).
- "a delivery team product owner was little more than a backlog administrator." (S046, 2024-01-30) "the senior engineer could do it better anyway." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "They're dramatically overpaid for the value they provide." (S001, 2024-03-10) That line is about feature-team product managers, not about the empowered role (S001).
- "In literally every truly strong product company I know, there are strong product leaders." (S033, 2020-11-22) "Probably the single most important, yet most often overlooked, element to capable management is coaching." (S023, 2018-10-31)

## Product ops
- **Stance:** The name means nothing until you see the job. The version he wants is a small force multiplier of user research, data analysts, and a senior person raising the skill of the teams. The versions he fights are a revived project office, a second product manager, or a leader who handed away coaching and strategy.
- "if a company says we have Product Ops, that's a fairly meaningless statement today." (S034, 2021-12-20)
- "the heart of product ops in the good companies I know is user research and data analysts." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "I feel like I have been playing whack-a-mole on this problem for literally twenty years." (S034, 2021-12-20) The mole is splitting the product manager (S034, S001).
- "That's a huge red flag and I try to tell people, if that's what you see, run." (S001, 2024-03-10)
- "using Product Ops as a Trojan Horse to bring back in the people, practices and especially the command and control mindset of the old style PMO." (S034, 2021-12-20)
- "these insights teams are there to help product teams and product leaders to make informed decisions, not to make those decisions for them." (S034, 2021-12-20)
- "there really isn't a good substitute for capable, engaged product leaders working one on one with their product managers" (S034, 2021-12-20)

## Four risks
- **Stance:** Judge value, usability, feasibility, and business viability before you build. The product manager owns value and viability, the designer usability, the tech lead feasibility. Value risk is usually the hardest. Customer love is not enough.
- **2008 through about 2017:** three risks, value, usability, and feasibility, with value supposed to cover the customer and the business. "I used this for well over a decade, and I like that it's just 3 risks, and it's fairly intuitive for people to grasp." (S042, 2023-07-10)
- **Newer, called out in 2017 and locked in the 2018 second edition:** four risks, because teams were skipping the business. "it was obscuring some pretty serious risks and challenges, and making it too easy for product managers to focus on customer value and overlook business value." (S021, 2017-12-04) "I now explicitly call out the four types of risk" (S021, 2017-12-04) "But I believed at the time, and I still believe this is the case, that it's worth the extra cognitive load to have the four risk taxonomy" (S042, 2023-07-10) He still prefers three items for memory, and he keeps four anyway (S042).
- "I still argue that the toughest risk is usually value risk" (S021, 2017-12-04)
- "there's a straight line between not using one of the risk taxonomies, and failed product efforts." (S042, 2023-07-10)
- IDEO's desirability, viability, and feasibility fits consumer products better than business products, because in a business the user and the buyer are different people. "I have found it to be a much better fit for consumer products, than it is for business products." (S042, 2023-07-10)
- He is sympathetic to splitting ethics, go-to-market, and compliance out of viability, and he leaves them inside so the list stays short enough to use (S042).
- "For a product to be viable, our marketing organization must be able to effectively market it, sales must be able to effectively sell, finance needs to be able to effectively fund and monetize" (S036, 2022-02-07) Legal and compliance sit in the same sentence (S036).
