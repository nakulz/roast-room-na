# Frameworks: Charlie Munger

19 frameworks, roughly most-used first, with the failure and mental-model tools a founder would actually use put at the front. "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. Inversion
- **What it is:** Solve the hard problem backward. Ask how to produce the failure, the misery, or the ruin, then do not do those things. He treats this as algebra applied to business and to a life, not as a slogan.
- **Parts:** State the bad outcome in concrete terms. List the few reliable ways to get it. Cross them out of the plan. Go back and forth between what you want and what you want to avoid. A special case is his avoidance rule: collect the standard stupidities and stay away from them.
- **When to use:** Any decision where the upside story is fluent and the failure story is vague. Pricing, a raise, a hire, a partnership, a new product, a debt load.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can name three specific ways this plan fails and has already blocked each one. 3 if the failure list is real but nothing in the plan changes because of it. 1 if the only story is how it succeeds.
- **Quote:** "It really works to tackle much of life by inversion, where you just twist the thing around backwards and answer it that way." (S018)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S004, S012, S017, S018, S022, S033, S034, S035, S039)

## 2. Circle of competence
- **What it is:** Every person has a small area where they have an edge, and knowing the boundary is the skill. Outside it, the right move is to decline, not to study harder at the last minute. He also calls the rejected pile the too hard pile.
- **Parts:** Name what you actually know. Name the edge. Put everything past the edge on the too hard pile and leave it there. Do not play where the other people are better. Pick the pond that still has fish, meaning a place where your edge is unusual, not a crowded game. Chauffeur knowledge, the talk without the work, does not count (S017).
- **When to use:** A new market, a technical product, a hire who will "figure it out," an investment or acquisition outside the founder's record, any pitch that starts with a field the founder cannot explain.
- **How to score:** 5 if they can say what they will not do, and why someone else is better at it. 3 if the edge is real but the plan quietly depends on a neighboring skill they do not have. 1 if the plan requires them to be wise where others are already specialists.
- **Quote:** "I don't play in a game where the other people are wise and I'm stupid. I look for a place where I'm wise and they're stupid." (S034)
- **Sources:** 13 (S003, S004, S018, S026, S028, S029, S030, S032, S033, S034, S035, S036, S039)
- **Where the line moved:** From 1994 through the mid-2010s he treated high tech, and airlines, as outside his edge (S003, S004). In 2017 he said Berkshire had bought Apple and airlines because the facts had changed, with only a modest edge (S030). In 2018 he said changing your mind when the facts change is allowed, and he still would not value Google, Facebook, Amazon, or Alibaba (S032). In 2020 he still left complicated technology to others (S034). By 2023 he owned Apple and said they had not totally failed at tech, while the early easy method no longer worked (S039). Newer rule: a changed industry can enter the circle. A business he still cannot value stays out.

## 3. Incentives
- **What it is:** People do what they are paid to do, including honest people, and they bend their beliefs to match the pay. Advice, forecasts, and "culture" come second.
- **Parts:** Ask who gets paid if the decision goes through, and who bears the loss if it fails. Discount the barber. Design the pay so the desired behavior is the easy one. Fear easy money, fee-driven buying, and any system that is simple to game. A lender, a broker, a venture fund, and a surgeon can all drift without feeling like crooks.
- **When to use:** Compensation, partnerships, channel deals, fundraising advice, a board, a pricing scheme, any vendor who is paid on volume.
- **How to score:** 5 if the person who decides also keeps the downside, and nobody is paid to push the wrong action. 3 if the incentives are mostly aligned but one advisor is paid only if you say yes. 1 if the plan needs someone to act against their paycheck.
- **Quote:** "If the incentives are wrong, the behavior will be wrong. I guarantee it." (S028)
- **Sources:** 16 (S003, S004, S012, S017, S022, S024, S025, S026, S028, S029, S030, S031, S032, S034, S038, S039)

## 4. Lollapalooza effects
- **What it is:** When two, three, or four forces point the same way, the result is not the sum. It blows past a breakpoint, for good or for ruin. He had the interaction of many psychological tendencies by 1994 (S003) and the name by 1996 (S004).
- **Parts:** List the forces already in motion: incentives, social proof, denial, a fad, easy credit, a feedback loop. If several point the same way, expect an extreme. The same stack can build a great business (a brand plus habit plus availability) or a bubble (rising prices, no money down, fees, and marks that hide the loss).
- **When to use:** A growth story that feels "inevitable," a culture that is getting worse fast, a product that is working better than one feature explains, a boom in the customer's industry.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can name three forces that truly point the same way, and the nasty version of that stack. 3 if only one force is real and the rest are adjectives. 1 if they predict a smooth, moderate outcome in a setup that is already stacked.
- **Quote:** "You get lollapalooza effects when two, three or four forces are all operating in the same direction." (S004)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S004, S012, S018, S025, S027, S030)

## 5. Opportunity cost
- **What it is:** A yes is a no to the best thing you could do with the same money, time, and attention. He thinks economics textbooks name the idea and then drop it (S012, S022).
- **Parts:** Name the best alternative already in hand. Reject the new idea in seconds if it is not better than that. Count management time, reputation, and the founder's attention, not just cash. A crowded pond fails this test even when the idea is clever (S032, S033).
- **When to use:** A side product, a second market, an acquisition, a hire that creates a new function, any use of the last free dollar or the last free afternoon.
- **How to score:** 5 if they can name the best current use and show why this beats it. 3 if they compare the idea with doing nothing. 1 if cash or boredom is the reason to proceed.
- **Quote:** "If we've got one thing we can do more of, we're not interested in anything that's not better than that." (S033)
- **Sources:** 5 (S012, S022, S029, S030, S033)

## 6. Bet seldom, then sit still *(one-foot fences)*
- **What it is:** The edge comes from a few easy, mispriced chances, taken large, and from doing nothing the rest of the time. Sitting in a great business also keeps the tax man from taking a cut every year. He looks for one-foot fences with a big reward, not seven-foot problems.
- **Parts:** Work and read so you can recognize the chance. Wait without forcing action. When it is clearly inside the circle and the price is right, bet heavily. Then hold. A lifetime may offer only a handful of these (he says four real pounces, or five or six times). Three holdings can be enough if each is truly understood. Wide activity is how people who feel useless lose money.
- **When to use:** Whether to launch, raise, acquire, or spend surplus cash. Also whether to keep tinkering with a business that already works.
- **How to score:** 5 if this is one of the few, the price is plain, and the founder can say what they will not do while they wait. 3 if the chance is real but the size of the bet is timid or the wait is being filled with noise. 1 if the motive is to feel active. Guidance, not a quote: years of buying nothing is a normal result of the rule, not a failure of it (S010, S011).
- **Quote:** "If you sit on your ass for long, long stretches in great companies, you can get a huge edge from nothing but the way income taxes work." (S003)
- **Sources:** 15 (S002, S003, S004, S010, S011, S024, S026, S027, S029, S030, S033, S035, S036, S038, S039)
- **Where the line moved:** In 1994 he still taught Ben Graham's gap between price and value, and he already said the big edge was sitting in great companies (S003). By 2015 the buying rule was a fair price for a good business a serious owner could understand, almost never sold (S027). In 2017 he said the old cigar-butt path, buying a lousy business because it was cheap, was a bad pattern to copy (S031). Newer rule: pay a fair price for a good business. A cheap rotten one is a special case, not the system.

## 7. The model checklist
- **What it is:** The big models have to be used on purpose, in order, the way a pilot uses a checklist. Smart people who trust their memory skip a force and then cannot see why the result was extreme.
- **Parts:** Keep a short list of the models that actually carry the weight: incentives, opportunity cost, scale, compound interest, feedback, the main psychological biases, breakage and backup, and basic probability. Run the situation down the list. Stop at the first item that changes the decision. He also uses narrower lists: ways a life fails (S001), and every defect that irritates a customer (S028).
- **When to use:** Before a yes on a complex deal, a launch, or a system other people will game. Also after a surprise, to see which item was skipped.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can show the list and the item that decided it. 3 if they have the concepts but did not actually run them. 1 if the decision was a feeling dressed up after the fact.
- **Quote:** "And without a checklist, you will screw up time after time, too." (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S012, S017, S028, S031)

## 8. Elementary worldly wisdom *(latticework of mental models)*
- **What it is:** About 80 or 90 models from many fields do most of the work of seeing a situation clearly, and a handful do the heavy work. One model is a hammer, and then every problem looks like a nail. The academic label on the idea does not matter. Use the most basic explanation and use it often enough that it becomes fluent.
- **Parts:** Take the main ideas from microeconomics, psychology, mathematics, engineering, and biology. Learn them well enough to use every day. When two fields conflict, reconcile them instead of hiding in one department. Spend a real slice of time, he says on the order of 10 or 20 percent, on the big ideas outside your job (S030). There is no formula that replaces the mix (S029).
- **When to use:** A problem that one department's jargon has already "solved." A founder who explains everything with one metric, one growth hack, or one ideology.
- **How to score:** 5 if two or three models from different fields agree, and none of the basic ones was skipped. 3 if the second model was found only after a push. 1 if a single framework is doing all the talking.
- **Quote:** "80 or 90 important models will carry about 90% of the freight in making you a worldly-wise person" (S003)
- **Sources:** 11 (S003, S004, S008, S012, S017, S018, S029, S030, S032, S035, S039)

## 9. The moat *(share of mind, scale, pricing power)*
- **What it is:** A good business keeps its economics when rivals try to take them. He cares more about share of mind than share of market, about untapped pricing power, and about advantages of scale that are real. He also wants a product people ask for by name, and a place so hard or so extreme that copycats stay out.
- **Parts:** Who would the customer miss, and why? Can price rise without volume collapsing? Does scale make you better at the work, or only bigger and dumber? Is the brand a habit, or a story? Is the niche sticky because it is agony for the giants (S029)? A fanatic low-cost, low-SKU operator (Costco is his model) can be the moat. Extreme quality plus the right incentives can be the moat. A moat you cannot explain is not one.
- **When to use:** Pricing, a competitor's entrance, a brand spend, a choice between a niche and a broad launch, a claim that "we will be the platform."
- **How to score:** 5 if customers already pay up or stay when a cheaper option exists, and the reason is hard to copy. 3 if the advantage is real but one well-funded rival could rent it away. 1 if the moat is a hope about the future brand.
- **Quote:** "So it is not share of market but share of mind that counts." (S004)
- **Sources:** 11 (S003, S004, S024, S026, S028, S029, S034, S035, S036, S038, S039)

## 10. Destroy your own best-loved ideas
- **What it is:** A conclusion is not ready until you have tried to kill it. Darwin is his model: give the disconfirming evidence priority. The strict form is his iron prescription. No opinion until you can state the other side better than the people who hold it.
- **Parts:** Write the case against the plan in the opponent's strongest form. Seek the fact that would hurt the idea, and look at it first. Reward yourself for dropping a loved idea. Rub your own nose in mistakes so the next one is less likely (S024, S035). When the facts change, change the conclusion in the open. Do not pound the old idea in by repeating it.
- **When to use:** Before a raise, a public commitment, a hire of a friend, or any plan the founder has already defended twice.
- **How to score:** 5 if they can state the opposing case better than a skeptic, and they have changed at least one piece of the plan because of it. 3 if they name a risk and then explain it away. 1 if disagreement is treated as disloyalty.
- **Quote:** "I'm not entitled to have an opinion on this subject unless I can state the arguments against my position better than the people do who are supporting it." (S017)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S004, S012, S017, S024, S028, S030, S031, S032, S034, S035, S036)

## 11. Margin of safety
- **What it is:** After you have done the odds, demand extra room for mischances, bad luck, and your own error. The idea is from engineering, by way of Graham. It shows up as cash, little debt, a cap on any one loss, and a refusal to let a lender's nerves force a sale.
- **Parts:** Keep liquidity on purpose. Match the life of assets and liabilities. Cap what one failure can cost. Assume the tails are fatter than a normal curve (S022). Do not build a position that only exists until someone asks for the money (he calls that good until reached for). A bridge with the spare steel removed is his picture of a firm levered to please outsiders (S028).
- **When to use:** Debt, a lease, a customer concentration, a single supplier, a bet that uses money the company cannot lose, any plan that works only if nothing is late.
- **How to score:** 5 if a bad year, a lost customer, or a down round still leaves the firm alive and unforced. 3 if survival depends on one friendly lender or one more round. 1 if the plan has no spare and calls that efficiency.
- **Quote:** "You want something that will handle some mischances from life and still do well." (S018)
- **Sources:** 10 (S003, S005, S018, S021, S022, S027, S028, S030, S032, S035)

## 12. Second-order effects
- **What it is:** The first result is often not the one that matters. Consequences have consequences. A cost cut, a new machine, a subsidy, or a clever contract can hand the gain to someone else, or set off a loop.
- **Parts:** Ask who gets the benefit after competitors copy the move. Ask what people will do once the new rule is in place. Watch feedback: rising prices that pull in more buyers, fees that pull in more product, a metric that people learn to fake. Stop at the step where the gain leaves the firm.
- **When to use:** Automation, a price cut "to gain share," a growth hack, a financing trick, a policy or a commission scheme that looks profitable in year one.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can say where the gain sits after rivals respond. 3 if they see the second step and still hope they will be the exception. 1 if the spreadsheet stops at the first saving.
- **Quote:** "the consequences have consequences, and the consequences of the consequences have consequences" (S012)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S008, S012, S025, S031)

## 13. Psychology of misjudgment *(two-track analysis)*
- **What it is:** A decision has two tracks. First the real interests, rationally stated. Then the subconscious pulls that bend judgment: incentive bias, denial, consistency, social proof, deprival (losses felt harder than equal gains), envy, and mere association. About twenty of these tendencies interact. Textbooks of his day left the important ones out (S004).
- **Parts:** State the rational case. Then ask which bias is already in the room. Denial keeps a sunk cost alive. Social proof makes a whole industry do a dumb thing together. Deprival makes people fight to keep a bad product, a bad price, or a bad identity. Envy makes rivals, and founders, irrational. If several fire at once, hand the case to the lollapalooza test.
- **When to use:** A founder who cannot drop a feature, a team copying a rival, a customer base that will hate a "better" change, any boom everyone now agrees on.
- **How to score:** 5 if the rational track and the bias track are both named, and the plan is changed where the bias is strongest. 1 if the case is only a spreadsheet, or only a story about how people "should" behave.
- **Quote:** "Failure to handle psychological denial is a common way for people to go broke" (S004)
- **Sources:** 9 (S003, S004, S008, S012, S017, S018, S022, S030, S032)

## 14. Cancer surgery formula
- **What it is:** In a sick business, find any part that can live on its own, cut the rest away, and if nothing sound remains, liquidate. Do not wait for a heroic manager to heal a rotten core. The same move applies to a fresh mistake: reverse it while the cost is embarrassment, not ruin.
- **Parts:** Separate the sound remnant from the mess. Cut. If the remnant cannot stand alone, exit the line. Do not average the sick part into a bigger total so the loss is harder to see. Take the hit that makes you look foolish.
- **When to use:** A product line, a location, a customer segment, or an acquisition that is melting. Also the moment the founder first knows a deal was wrong.
- **How to score:** 5 if they can point to the sound piece and have a date to cut or exit the rest. 3 if they are "watching it" with no cut list. 1 if the plan is a new executive who will fix a business whose economics are broken.
- **Quote:** "They look at this mess. And they figure out if there's anything sound left that can live on its own if they cut away everything else." (S003)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S021, S031, S038)

## 15. How the business dies *(technological destruction, no bounce-back)*
- **What it is:** Companies die more like species than like machines that a clever owner can keep forever. New technology and hard competition hand the gains to customers, wipe out incumbents, and turn yesterday's moat into a commodity. A structural decline does not bounce back when the economy does. His preferred answer is to avoid that game, not to out-invent it.
- **Parts:** Ask what kills this business: a cheaper substitute, a change in how the customer buys, a rival who works harder, a commodity price that never recovers. If the lost volume is structural, do not model a rebound. If the firm is in the path of that change, get out or do not enter. A position that is hard to take away is worth more than a clever fight in a melting one.
- **When to use:** A turnaround, a "temporary" revenue drop, a tech shift in the customer's industry, a founder who says the brand will carry them through a new channel.
- **How to score:** 5 if they can name the destroyer and either they are off that path or they have a reason the destroyer does not apply. 1 if the forecast needs the old volume to return, or needs the firm to win a technology race it has not won before.
- **Quote:** "We don't solve the problem of technological obsolescence, we avoid it." (S026)
- **Sources:** 11 (S003, S016, S019, S021, S023, S026, S028, S034, S035, S036, S039)
- **Limit he states:** He missed the speed of the newspaper decline himself (S032). Avoiding change is not the same as seeing the date. The rule still bars a plan that needs a dead channel to recover.

## 16. Expect the bad year
- **What it is:** Unfavorable surprises are the ones that matter, because favorable ones are easy. Arrange the firm, the balance sheet, and the founder's life so a bad year does not force a stupid decision. He includes a large drop in the price of something you own.
- **Parts:** Say in advance what a horrible year looks like. Hold enough liquidity to live through it. Do not promise smooth results in a lumpy business. Practice the 50 percent decline without having to sell or to quit. Anticipate trouble so it is not a shock when it arrives (S017).
- **When to use:** A budget, a covenant, a personal spending level tied to the company's paper value, any story that needs next year to be kind.
- **How to score:** 5 if a bad year is already in the plan and the firm can sit through it. 1 if one bad year breaks payroll, the covenant, or the founder's nerve. Guidance: he was also early and too grim on some macro trouble, including inflation (S028). Readiness is the test, not a forecast of the date.
- **Quote:** "conduct your life so that you can handle the 50% decline with aplomb and grace." (S030)
- **Sources:** 8 (S005, S006, S009, S010, S017, S024, S028, S030)

## 17. The high road *(sell what you would buy)*
- **What it is:** The durable way to get what you want is to deserve it. Sell what you would buy if you were the customer. Do not take the last dollar from people in distress, and do not ship a trick. He treats this as practical, because trust is an asset other people cannot rent.
- **Parts:** Would you buy it on these terms? Does the price of the symbol of value stay low on purpose, the way he describes Costco's iconic item? Are you paid only when the thing works, rather than for hours or for a cost-plus pad? Leave easy but dirty profit alone. A seamless web of deserved trust, his picture of a Mayo operating room, is the culture he wants (S017, S039).
- **When to use:** Pricing, refunds, a hard sell, a fee, a contract the customer cannot understand, a boom in which you could raise prices on people who are stuck.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder would take the other side of the same deal, and the firm gets paid for the result. 1 if the profit requires confusion, distress, or a customer who will not come back.
- **Quote:** "sell what you would buy if you were the buyer, not what you can sell by misleading people." (S039)
- **Sources:** 10 (S017, S020, S024, S026, S030, S032, S034, S036, S038, S039)

## 18. The Berkshire system *(tiny headquarters, no buying department)*
- **What it is:** A set of operating choices that keep a firm from the usual ways acquirers and bureaucracies destroy value. He wrote them out as a system in 2015 (S027). It is not a template he thinks every company should copy.
- **Parts:** Almost no headquarters. Extreme autonomy for operators who already love the business. No department of acquisitions under pressure to buy, and no advisors paid to prefer a deal. Buy with cash, at a fair price, a business the buyer understands, with a good chief already in place. Almost never sell. Pay no dividend while each retained dollar creates more than a dollar of value. Little debt, so credit stays intact when a rare chance appears. Look at problems as an owner, not as a careerist protecting a job (S031). Bureaucracy, in his line, should be treated like a cancer.
- **When to use:** A founder building a second layer of management, a corp-dev hire, a roll-up, a decision to sell a good subsidiary, a headquarters that is growing faster than the work.
- **How to score:** 5 if capital allocation sits with a few owners, operators are trusted, and nobody is employed to do deals. 1 if a staff exists to buy things, or if headquarters talent is the plan for fixing weak units. He says the system was partly stumbled into, and that it is not one size for every firm (S027).
- **Quote:** "It never had the equivalent of a department of acquisitions under pressure to buy." (S027)
- **Sources:** 6 (S004, S027, S031, S032, S033, S039)

## 19. Roughly right, not precisely wrong
- **What it is:** A number that looks exact and is economically empty will beat a rough number that is true, unless you throw the exact one out. Accounting, models, and forecasts all do this. He would rather be roughly right.
- **Parts:** Ignore gains that are not cash and not a change in the business. Ignore charges that are only a rulebook entry. If a model produces an insane result, the model is wrong. Distrust false precision, EBITDA used as a substitute for earnings, and projections made to sell a deal. Do not forecast the economy in order to justify holding cash or spending it (S002, S018, S021).
- **When to use:** A board pack, an acquisition model, a compensation metric, a fund pitch, any number with several decimals and a hopeful story.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can state the economic result in one rough figure and can say which reported number they are ignoring. 1 if the decision needs the precise model to be true, or needs a macro forecast.
- **Quote:** "Better roughly right than precisely wrong." (S012)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S009, S012, S015, S018, S022, S034, S038)
