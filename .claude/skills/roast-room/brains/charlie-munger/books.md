# Books: Charlie Munger

Eleven books. His decision rules live mostly in talks and letters, which are the corpus; book text was not fetched. Summaries are in my own words and say what he uses each book for. Darwin is a working method in these notes, not a volume he assigns. Titles that show up only as a reading list, with no decision use, are left out at the end.

## Poor Charlie's Almanack (2005, Peter Kaufman)
- **Core thesis:** A collection of his talks, assembled by Kaufman from the files. He did not plan the book. The use he states is the same method he credits to Lee Kuan Yew: find what works and do it, find what does not work and avoid it.
- **Key frameworks:** Inversion as a life habit. A lattice of the big ideas, used until they are fluent. Deserved trust, incentives, and the refusal to play games where others are better. In 2021 he said he had no wonderful new thoughts to add, and that the old ways still worked.
- **Taught in sources:** S033, S036, S039.

## Principles of Economics (N. Gregory Mankiw, 1998)
- **Core thesis:** A freshman textbook. He bought it when it was new because the advance was large, and he went through it to see what was being taught.
- **Key frameworks:** He pulls out two ideas and treats them as the ones that decide: opportunity cost, and incentives. He says both are superpowers if you actually use them. He also complains that the book takes models from other fields without saying whose they are. Years later he says opportunity cost gets about one sentence near the front of a long economics text and then disappears. Use: do not let schooling bury the two questions that should kill a deal in seconds. What else could this money and this hour do, and who is paid if we say yes?
- **Taught in sources:** S012, S022.

## The Tragedy of the Commons (Garrett Hardin, 1968)
- **Core thesis:** A shared resource is ruined when each person takes a rational private share. He likes Hardin's wicked invisible foot set next to Adam Smith's invisible hand.
- **Key frameworks:** Any system where each actor's gain is the group's loss will be gamed. Over-lending, fee harvesting, fraud that spreads once it pays, and a workplace that rewards the person who grabs more than the place can stand. The fix is a rule or an owner who feels the damage, not a hope that people will be nicer than their pay.
- **Taught in sources:** S012.

## F.I.A.S.C.O. (Frank Partnoy, 1997)
- **Core thesis:** An insider's account of derivatives trading. He says it will turn your stomach.
- **Key frameworks:** A legal, credentialed market can still be a casino with the house edge. Complexity and a prestigious employer are not evidence that the product is safe, or that the customer understands it. Use: if the business only works because the other side does not see the terms, do not own it and do not copy it.
- **Taught in sources:** S012.

## The Fable of the Bees (Bernard Mandeville, 1714)
- **Core thesis:** Private vices can produce a busy commercial life. He uses the book as a model of envy.
- **Key frameworks:** Envy and display push spending, so a vice can raise measured activity and still be a stupid way to live and a bad thing to sell. Do not confuse a boom in purchases with a sound customer need. Envy remains, in his later talks, a motive that ruins judgment even when it moves money.
- **Taught in sources:** S012.

## The Language Instinct (Steven Pinker, 1994)
- **Core thesis:** A large part of the capacity for language is inborn. He agrees, and he agrees with the attack on standard psychology textbooks as a bad map of the mind.
- **Key frameworks:** Some mental habits are built in. A lecture will not remove them, which is why incentive, social proof, and denial have to be designed around rather than argued away. The textbook is the wrong checklist. Build your own from what actually bends behavior.
- **Taught in sources:** S004.

## The Rational Optimist (Matt Ridley, 2010)
- **Core thesis:** A case for progress through trade and the division of labor. He has read it several times and thinks it is a good book.
- **Key frameworks:** He uses it as a warning against a single cause. Even a writer he admires can jump too fast to one explanation and miss the rest of the stack. Division of labor matters. It is not the whole model. If a plan or a book explains a complex result with one force, keep looking.
- **Taught in sources:** S024.

## Guns, Germs, and Steel (Jared Diamond, 1997)
- **Core thesis:** He calls it one of the great books and says the main point is wrong. The book argues that geography and the available plants and animals, not differences among peoples, explain which societies grew rich and armed.
- **Key frameworks:** He will not take a famous explanation whole. His objection is that human populations keep changing after civilization starts, so a story that stops at geography is incomplete. Use: a brilliant single-cause history is still a hammer. Check the claim against the next fact, and be willing to keep the book and drop the thesis.
- **Taught in sources:** S018.

## Titan (Ron Chernow, 1998)
- **Core thesis:** A biography of John D. Rockefeller. He uses it for the picture of a partner, not for a corporate strategy.
- **Key frameworks:** An admirable controlling partner puts up the money, takes the loss, and lets the others buy him out at cost if the venture works. The same record, in his reading, includes large giving. Use: when a founder or a backer wants control, ask whether they also take the downside, or only the upside and the credit.
- **Taught in sources:** S026.

## The Economic Consequences of the Peace (John Maynard Keynes, 1919)
- **Core thesis:** Keynes argued against crushing Germany with repayment demands after the first world war. He says that lesson showed up later in the choice to rebuild rather than to collect a ruinous debt.
- **Key frameworks:** A punishment that feels justified can wreck the next stretch of years. Second-order effects dominate the first moral satisfaction. Use: a contract, a firing, or a competitive blow that leaves the other side with nothing may cost more than the win was worth. He is applying a historical lesson, not offering a foreign policy.
- **Taught in sources:** S024.

## Capital in the Twenty-First Century (Thomas Piketty, 2014)
- **Core thesis:** A long argument, from historical numbers, that returns on capital tend to outrun general growth and so widen inequality. He says the figures may be right and the reading of them is not.
- **Key frameworks:** A famous data book can still hand you the wrong decision. Separate the count from the story told about the count. He does not treat the book as a guide to how a firm should price, hire, or invest. Use: do not let a thick book of tables close a question that the basic models have not been run on.
- **Taught in sources:** S026.

Titles named in the notes and left out, because they are a reading list or a passing mention with no decision use: The Selfish Gene (Dawkins), Living Within Limits (Hardin), Only the Paranoid Survive (Grove), Of Human Bondage (Maugham), The Pilgrim's Progress, A Man for All Markets (Thorp), The Outsiders (Thorndike), and The Money Masters (Train), the last of which is an editor's link rather than his own discussion.
