# Stories: Charlie Munger

18 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **Harvard School, the misery speech.** In June 1986 he copied the shape of a Johnny Carson commencement talk and filled it with ways to guarantee a failed life: unreliability, envy, resentment, learning only from your own scars, staying down after a reverse, and refusing to turn the question around. He took the method into business decisions. Study how to get the bad result, then stay away from that path. "many hard problems are best solved only when they are addressed backward" (S001) "I wish I knew where I was going to die, and then I’d never go there." (S001)

2. **See's Candy.** Berkshire bought the boxed-chocolate company out of a death-tax sale, about $20 million for a business earning about $4 million pretax, and paid a finder's fee once (S038). Price could go up about 10 percent a year with no loss of volume, and that lesson changed what Berkshire was willing to own. Blue Chip Stamps, which held the float, found only three uses for it across decades: See's, the Buffalo News, and Wesco (S024). He took patience plus a loved product with unused pricing power as the model. "we could raise the price every year 10%, and nobody cared." (S038) "Patience combined with opportunity is a great thing to have." (S024)

3. **Coca-Cola, invented on the blackboard.** At Stanford he builds the company from a blank page: a cold drink, a trademark, a habit, caffeine and sugar, no aftertaste, signs given away, the product always in reach, and a franchise that keeps control. The point of the exercise is that the owners get rich from the name people ask for. He also treats the old perpetual fixed syrup price as insane, because it assumed the currency would never inflate, and New Coke as a case of brilliant people ignoring how hard humans fight a loss. Coke reversed fast. "We will never get very rich from this business except by using a trademark and creating a brand strong enough that people order our new beverage by its trademarked name." (S004) "ever-fixed syrup price can ruin you" (S004) "people react to a minus five-unit force way more negatively than they react positively to a force of five unit plus" (S004)

4. **Federal Express, the night shift.** The night shift had been paid by the hour, and the planes were not getting loaded on time. Pay was switched to the shift, so the crew could go home when the work was done, and the lateness vanished overnight. "As usual, in human affairs, what wins are incentives." (S003)

5. **Wells Fargo and the cross-sell.** Aggressive goals pushed some employees to open accounts customers did not want. He treats the incentive design as an easy mistake, of the kind Teledyne also made under a very smart chief executive, where a few subsidiaries cheated. The larger failure was reading the bad news too slowly. Separate from that scandal, the 2009 Wesco letter says a large purchase of Wells Fargo and US Bancorp was timed about as badly as it could have been, while the 2014 meeting describes a Wells purchase near the bottom because it was cheap. Those are two tellings of bank stock, and the incentive failure is the later one. "Of course Wells Fargo had incentive systems that were too strong in the wrong direction." (S032) "The one nice thing about doing something dumb is that you probably won’t do it again." (S030) (S021, S024, S026, S030, S032, S036)

6. **The Salomon general counsel.** The lawyer told the chief executive that reporting the misconduct was a moral duty. The chief executive waited. The delay became a scandal, and both men lost their jobs. He retells it at the 2007 law commencement. The lesson he uses is practical: show the person the threat to his own interest, because a moral lecture did not move him. "appealing to interest is likely to work better as a matter of human persuasion than appeal to anything else" (S004) (S017)

7. **Wesco and the savings and loan.** Mutual Savings had a sound balance sheet and a mediocre business. Growth in that era was a way to lose money, and the rules made it hard for a careful operator to earn a respectable profit while reckless peers were rewarded. Management looked for a remedy for years and said it had not found one. A tag-end holding from those days, Freddie Mac, bought for $72 million, was worth about $1.2 billion in the 1997 letter and about $1.4 billion when the shares were sold in 2000. He also took one open venture bet inside that world, the Bowery, on about a 2 percent down payment, and said the chance of gain justified the chance of loss. "the regulatory structure of the savings and loan business creates a competitive situation in which it is hard to make respectable profits through careful operations" (S002) "continues to be a mediocre business from the shareholders' point of view" (S002) (S005, S009)

8. **The 2008-2009 crisis.** Before the break he was already calling that AAA paper a deeply immoral way to build a financial instrument, and a common-mode failure, risks that look separate and then fail together. He also refused assets that are only good until someone reaches for them. In February 2009 he wrote that the situation was dire, that moderate booms and busts are normal, and that the gross version was made of weak bank rules, bad conduct, leverage, consumer-credit excess, unsound accounting, and derivatives that gave people a reason to want firms to fail. Wesco's own $650 million in Wells Fargo and US Bancorp was bought into the crash. At Harvard-Westlake in 2010 he said he had known a crash was coming and had not known when, and that Berkshire's popularity was just the habit of avoiding stupidity. "This is a deeply immoral way to create financial instruments." (S018) "Our situation is dire." (S020) "The timing of our recent investments could not have been much worse." (S021) "It’s just avoiding stupidity." (S022)

9. **Daily Journal.** He bought a small legal-newspaper company. The 2014 meeting puts the price at $2.7 million, back in three or four years, and calls that pretty good for a failing business. The 2021 meeting puts the price at $2.5 million, with a dividend of that amount soon after. The internet then took the print franchise. At the 2014 meeting he said obsolescence had happened to it and that he had failed the holders. The replacement, software for courts, is the venture bet he says he hates: long sales cycles, odds against it, write-offs as they go, and a niche so unpleasant that large software firms stay out. Once won, it sticks. In the foreclosure boom the company had about an 80 percent share and could have raised prices by about $50 million. It did not. "Obsolescence is what's happened to the Daily Journal." (S026) "I've failed you here at the Daily Journal. I'm sorry." (S026) "It was really, as I've said again and again, a venture capital-type activity, which I hate and isn't why I made my way in life." (S028) "I don’t think capitalism requires that you make all the money that you can." (S030) (S029, S036)

10. **Costco.** Sol Price's model, which Jim Sinegal carried on, is the business he tells people to study: fewer items, fast turnover, suppliers financing the inventory, a membership that keeps out the behavior you do not want, and a fanatic hold on cost and quality. Walmart could not copy it, because the old idea was already sitting in the space. The hot dog price is the symbol, and Sinegal would not raise it. He says the two of them would not have treated that price as a board matter. "Yes, culture plus model." (S038) "Get it low and keep it there forever." (S038) "Those two would not have thought it was a board matter" (S038) "A business should be careful in the business it deliberately does without" (S039)

11. **The new loom.** Buffett wanted a labor-saving loom in a textile mill to fail. In a commodity business the cost saving is competed away and ends up with the buyer. After 20 years of those improvements the return on capital was still about 4 percent a year. He uses the same test whenever a cost cut in a commodity business is competed away. "It's just that the savings didn't go to you." (S003) "after 20 years of doing it, somehow you've earned a return of only about 4% per annum" (S003) (S012)

12. **Dexter Shoe.** Berkshire bought a shoe company and paid with Berkshire stock. Chinese competition destroyed the business right after. He calls it a total failure, and he files it under paying a liberal price. "We utterly failed on that one." (S024) (S039)

13. **CORT, bought at the top.** In February 2000, near the peak of the dot-com boom, Wesco paid $384 million for a furniture-rental company earning $46 million pretax, about eight times. New orders then fell, the recession and September 11 hit, and after-tax operating income dropped hard. Later letters repeat that he had been a poor predictor of near-term industry prospects. He did not hide the miss. CORT later used the down cycle to buy small businesses and cut debt by $30 million over two years. "Thus, in essence, Wesco paid $384 million for $46 million in pre-tax earnings." (S007) (S009, S010, S013, S019)

14. **Crypto, and the old ban on new shares.** He wanted nothing to do with bitcoin by 2017, called it poison in 2018, and in a 2023 Wall Street Journal piece said a cryptocurrency is not a currency, not a commodity, and not a security. It is a gambling contract with nearly a 100 percent edge for the house, helped by promoters who receive a block for almost nothing. He points at two precedents for a ban: China's ban, and England after the early-1700s crash, when Parliament stopped public trading in new common stocks for about a century while the country still led the Industrial Revolution. The later line is firmer, and it is the same hostility. "I think it's perfectly asinine to even pause to think about them." (S031) "a gambling contract with a nearly 100% edge for the house" (S037) "I think cryptocurrency ought to have been driven out as illegal." (S039) (S032, S036)

15. **The 1962 oil-royalty auction.** Only the ragged brokers showed up to bid, so he bid high enough to get some. A $1,000 outlay paid about $100,000 a year for 50 years. He tells it as a once-in-a-life chance. The lesson is to do something when one of your few chances arrives, because it will not come in a stream. "The trick in life is when you get the one, or two, or three that your fair allotment for a life is that you’ve got to do something about it." (S029)

16. **Belridge Oil, the miss.** He turned down a second block of shares in an oil company worth about three times its price. The 2014 meeting puts that omission at roughly $300 to $400 million, and he uses the story to say a person can survive a few dumb decisions. The 2015 Berkshire letter puts the huge errors in the same place: purchases that were not made, Walmart among them. "almost all huge errors were in not making a purchase, including not purchasing Walmart stock when that was sure to" (S027) (S026)

17. **William Miller Instruments.** His first investment was a Pasadena instrument maker. Magnetic tape, invented out of his sight, beat the product, and they sold three units. He took two lessons he still uses: a new technology can kill a business as easily as it can open one, and that scare is a reason to stay out of venture bets at the edge of a technology. In the same 2020 conversation he describes Burlington Northern the other way, a position nobody can easily rebuild. "We made that success not by conquering change, but by avoiding it." (S035)

18. **Kansas Bankers, leaving the line.** A bank failure cost the insurer $4.7 million, and he was less confident in deposit-guarantee bonds after that. Kansas Bankers stopped writing the bonds in September 2008 and cut the outstanding face from $9.7 billion to $3.4 billion, with the after-tax risk on any one bank failure capped at $7.6 million. By the 2009 letter the book was down to $33 million of face and 10 institutions, and the combined ratio had run at 140.2 percent. He treated the failure mode as a reason to get out, rather than to wait for the old profits to return. "Kansas Bankers discontinued writing deposit guarantee bonds, and in September 2008 it began to exit this line of insurance as rapidly as feasible." (S021) (S023)
