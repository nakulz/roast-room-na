# Stories: Andrew Ng

20 cases from the notes on S001, S002, S003, S005, and S006. The data lessons he uses to choose the next month of work come first. Each entry is the case, the lesson, and the source ids.

1. **Website cats, phone photos.** A cat detector trained on website photos, split 70/30, did well on that test set and failed on phone photos from real users. Lesson: draw the dev and test sets from the data you will actually see (S001).
2. **Cats split by region.** US and India sat in the dev set, China and the other regions in the test set, so the team would have tuned to two markets and been scored on two others. Lesson: dev and test have to come from one distribution (S001).
3. **A month on dogs.** A teammate wanted a month, and third-party software, to stop dogs being labeled as cats. Lesson: count the dog errors first. A 5% share is a low ceiling. A 50% share can move the number (S001).
4. **The accurate model that let bad images through.** A cat classifier with higher accuracy still let occasional pornographic images through. Lesson: when the metric ranks the wrong system first, change the metric (S001).
5. **Anti-spam, after years in the field.** Even with long experience he could not choose up front among honeypot data, text features, and header features. Lesson: build a basic system and let the errors choose, including in a field you know (S001).
6. **Speech that failed in cars.** Most of the bad dev clips were recorded in cars. Lesson: name what differs, then get training data that matches, including noise you synthesize with care (S001).
7. **About twenty video-game cars.** Synthetic car images looked real to people and came from about twenty designs. Lesson: a set that looks realistic to a person can still be narrow enough for the model to memorize (S001).
8. **Portland house prices.** On living area against price, a line underfits and a fifth-order curve hits the training points while predicting badly. On the 47-house set, adding bedrooms made the bedroom coefficient negative. Lesson: the goal is a prediction that holds on new houses, and the features you choose change the answer (S005, S006).
9. **The helicopter that could not find itself.** With Pieter Abbeel, two GPS units and about a year of FPGA work failed to localize the helicopter while it flew upside down. Cameras on the ground, with Adam Coates, solved localization so the team could move on to reinforcement learning. Lesson: a stuck measurement can block the work that matters (S003).
10. **Autorotation and the reward.** When a learned helicopter lands worse than a human pilot, compare the reward of the human trajectory with the reward of the machine trajectory. Lesson: that comparison shows whether to fix the reward or the learning algorithm (S001).
11. **A self-driving pipeline that was fine in pieces.** Each part was near human level and the car was not, because the planner never received lane markings. Lesson: when every component is close to a person and the whole system is far behind, the pipeline is missing information and has to be redesigned (S001).
12. **A team of doctors at 2%.** The example gives four human error levels, ending at 2% for a debating team of doctors. Lesson: once the system is already good, the reference is the best team you can assemble (S001).
13. **Speech, then house numbers, then ads.** At Google Brain the first internal customer was the speech team, the second was Maps reading house numbers off Street View, and only after those two wins did he open a serious conversation with Ads. Lesson: a company gets good at machine learning by winning a small project that earns trust (S003).
14. **Inspectors and a plastic pot.** Two inspectors disagree on the same scratch, and one inspector reverses between morning and afternoon. Lesson: with a few hundred labels, that noise is a large share of the data, so settle the labeling rules before you average (S003).
15. **The bird, and the tree that changes the light.** A bird flew through a factory and soiled what the camera saw. He also pictures a tree cut down outside, so it no longer shades a window and the lighting shifts. Lesson: a test set goes stale as soon as the environment moves (S003).
16. **The shared-credit button.** A feature let two people log in together at a San Francisco school and share credit. Zero people used it. They wanted to watch alone. Lesson: put the feature in front of real users before you invest in it (S003).
17. **Adam Coates's chart.** Bigger models kept doing better on his Stanford plot, and that figure was the conviction behind the Google Brain pitch. Senior friends called going for scale a bad career move. Lesson: the scale bet is the part he stands by, and it took conviction before the idea was popular (S003).
18. **The napkin in 2005.** After a conference, Hinton sketched synapses against seconds of life and made unsupervised learning look inevitable. Told in 2020, Ng calls the early Google Brain emphasis on unsupervised learning the thing they got wrong, and calls scale the thing they got right. Lesson: a compelling argument can still be early. The newer judgment is the 2020 one (S003).
19. **The video idea he cut.** An AI Fund idea would have had people watch many more videos in one narrow category. The business case and the revenue case were fine. He cut the idea. Lesson: he will drop a project whose revenue case works when he does not want more of that product in the world (S003).
20. **Videos that started at home.** While a professor he recorded education videos at home as a hobby, and that became the online education work (S002). The first large course already had about 100,000 people signed up and no videos yet, so he recorded between 10 p.m. and 3 a.m. on Fridays and Saturdays (S003). Lesson: a small side project can become the work, and the launch that looked sudden had taken years (S002, S003).
