# Principles: Andrew Ng

28 decision rules, roughly most-used first. Built from the notes on all 16 sources (S001 to S016). "Sources" counts distinct sources that state or apply the rule. Confidence: high means 3 or more sources, medium means 2, low means 1.

1. **More data fixes variance, and a model that cannot represent the pattern will not be saved by more data.** "If you have high variance, add data to your training set." (S001) "even if we were fitting a linear model to a very large amount of training data, the linear model would still fail to accurately capture the structure in the data." (S005) Adding training data helps variance and usually has no significant effect on bias (S001). In theory a massive training set can drive variance near zero (S001). Older (2012, S005): a larger hypothesis class can only lower bias, and it raises variance, so the examples you need grow roughly linearly with the number of parameters. Newer (2018, S001): with a well-designed regularization method you can usually increase the model size without increasing overfitting. Use the 2018 rule on a regularized neural net. Use the 2012 rule when the family is the wrong shape. S003 (2020): for many problems still far from Bayes error, bigger datasets with neural nets are enough without a new algorithm, and both scale and algorithms matter once you are close. S006: with enough data, feature choice becomes less critical. Sources: 4 (S001, S003, S005, S006). Confidence: high.
2. **Rank candidates on data they were not trained on, and use training error only as a bias reading.** "This algorithm does not work." (S004) "Fitting a 5th order polynomial to the data (rightmost figure) did not result in a good model." (S005) Training error always favors the most complex model (S004). A perfect pass through the training points is not the goal (S006). In the 2018 workflow the dev set ranks models, and training error is the bias number, not the selection number (S001). Sources: 4 (S001, S004, S005, S006). Confidence: high.
3. **With a small training set, features and human insight beat the choice of algorithm, and with a large set the reliable lever is a bigger network plus more data.** "the hand-engineering of features will have a bigger effect than the choice of algorithm." (S001) "But one of the more reliable ways to improve an algorithm’s performance today is still to (i) train a bigger network and (ii) get more data." (S001) Around 20 examples, hand-built features dominate. Around a million examples, he favors the neural net (S001, 2018). A very wide, short table should be cut down before the fit (S004). Do not add features just because you can (S006). With little data, a model with stronger assumptions can win because it is more data efficient; with a very large non-Gaussian set, the weaker model almost always wins (S007). When data is scarce, most of what the system knows has to come from human insight (S001). Sources: 4 (S001, S004, S006, S007). Confidence: high.
4. **Draw dev and test data from the future you care about, and keep dev and test on one distribution.** "Choose dev and test sets to reflect data you expect to get in the future and want to do well on." (S001) "Having mismatched dev and test sets makes it harder to figure out what is and isn’t working, and thus makes it harder to prioritize what to work on." (S001) Do not assume the training distribution is the test distribution (S001). A benchmark whose dev and test disagree rewards luck more than skill (S001). S005 treats same-distribution train and test, and independent draws, as the assumptions the theory actually needs. S003 (2020): deployment moves the distribution anyway, so a set that matched last quarter can go stale. Sources: 3 (S001, S003, S005). Confidence: high.
5. **Count the share of current errors a project can touch before you spend a month on it.** "you find that only 5% of the misclassified images are dogs, then no matter how much you improve your algorithm’s performance on dog images, you won’t get rid of more than 5% of your errors." (S001) "It often feels more exciting to just jump in and implement some idea, rather than question if the idea is worth the time investment." (S001) A hand count of about 100 mistakes is a couple of hours against a month of building (S001). S002 treats error analysis as a main driver of data-centric progress. S003 treats the same habit as the difference between a six-month dead end and a fast debug. Choosing poorly costs months (S001). Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S003). Confidence: high.
6. **Test whether more data can move the number before you spend months collecting it.** "It could be a waste of time to work on getting more data." (S001) "you could run some tests and could have figured out six months earlier that for this particular problem, collecting more data isn't going to cut it." (S003) If training error is already far from the target, more data is the wrong project (S001). A learning curve that has flattened is enough to stop a plan to double the set (S001). S005 agrees from the other direction: more data shrinks variance, and it cannot repair a line fit to curved data. Sources: 3 (S001, S003, S005). Confidence: high.
7. **Set optimal error from the best human performance you can actually get before you choose bias work or variance work.** "In this case, I would use 2% as the human-level performance proxy for our optimal error rate." (S001) "However, the problems that the two models suffer from are very different." (S005) A junior at 10% is the wrong proxy once a team of doctors can reach 2% (S001). High error from underfitting and high error from overfitting look alike and need different fixes (S005). Knowing the optimal rate is what makes the next step obvious (S001). S003 (2020): a few sets may already be near Bayes error or past humans, and then both new algorithms and more data have less room. Sources: 3 (S001, S003, S005). Confidence: high.
8. **Use the stronger model when you trust the shape of the data, and the weaker model when you do not.** "GDA makes stronger modeling assumptions, and is more data efficient" (S007) "Logistic regression makes weaker assumptions, and is significantly more robust to deviations from modeling assumptions." (S007) Strong assumptions buy data efficiency and lose robustness. Weak assumptions do the opposite (S007). Pick the family from the kind of target rather than by habit (S006). Newer application (2018, S001): a big flexible net can safely mix sources that share one mapping, and a small model cannot. Sources: 3 (S001, S006, S007). Confidence: high.
9. **Keep several tools on the table, and let a held-out comparison plus a simple baseline beat a fashionable default.** "some days we use a neural net, some days we use a PCA." (S003) "the Naive Bayes classifier often works surprisingly well." (S007) Older (2015, S008): the SVM notes place support vector machines among the best off-the-shelf supervised learners, and say many people think they are the best. Newer (2020, S003): the team should not use only deep learning. S004 applies one held-out procedure to very different families, including an SVM, a neural net, and logistic regression. The 2018 scale rule still applies inside deep learning: bigger network, more data (S001). It does not license a one-model team. Sources: 3 (S003, S004, S007). Confidence: high.
10. **Build a basic system in days, and let its measured errors choose the next step.** "So don’t start off trying to design and build the perfect system." (S001) "So it makes sense to build an end-to-end system quickly and revise it until it works well." (S002) The 2022 instruction to build an end-to-end system quickly means a working slice of the product. It does not mean one model from raw input to final output. inferred from S001, S002. Even deep experience, his own on anti-spam, did not let him pick the right direction before there were errors to read (S001). S003: a feature he shipped because it sounded right had zero users. Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S003). Confidence: high.
11. **Study first when the shot is costly or hard to undo, and fire first when the shot is cheap.** "When I was younger, I used to execute on the first idea I was excited about." (S002) "Ready, Aim, Fire tends to be superior when the cost of execution is high and a study can shed light on how useful or valuable a project could be." (S002) Newer (2022, S002): brainstorm more than the first exciting idea, take many shots when they are cheap, and spend more time up front on one-way doors. For the model itself he still fires first, because a study will not name the model, the data, or the hyperparameters (S002). S001 (2018) is that fire-first build. S003 (2020) is the aim-first company check: customer calls, and proof the technology can be built. inferred from S001, S002, S003. A month of debate on a one-week project is the wrong trade (S002). Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S003). Confidence: high.
12. **Start with a small project that lets the team learn, because starting too big is the more common failure.** "I've seen a lot more companies fail by starting too big than by starting too small." (S003) "Don’t worry about starting too small." (S002) At Google Brain, speech came before Maps house numbers, and ads came after those wins (S003). S002 stacks projects the same way: small, then valuable, then larger, with each win pulling resources. S001 applies it to the first system: a basic build is worth reading even when it is far from the best system you can build. Sources: 3 (S001, S002, S003). Confidence: high.
13. **Count generated data as useful only after it matches the distribution you will be judged on.** "When synthesizing data, put some thought into whether you’re really synthesizing a representative set of examples." (S001) "I don't think there's a whole enchilada. I think it's just a piece of it." (S003) Data that looks real to a person can still look fake to the model, and a narrow source, one hour of noise or 20 car models, is something the learner can memorize (S001). His teams have needed weeks to get the details close enough to matter (S001). The second quote is his 2020 view of self-supervised learning: a way to make unlimited labels, and only a piece. Measure the downstream task. inferred from S001, S003. Sources: 2 (S001, S003). Confidence: medium.
14. **When train and dev differ, name the difference and get training data that matches the failures.** "Try to understand what properties of the data differ between the training and the dev set distributions." (S001) "we don't know how to deal with test set distributions that are dramatically different than the training set distribution." (S003) Use a training-dev set so you do not confuse mismatch with variance (S001). There is no guarantee the matching data exists (S001). Newer limit (2020, S003): a dramatic shift is still unsolved, so do not treat transfer learning as a finished answer. The 2018 steps remain the work. Sources: 2 (S001, S003). Confidence: medium.
15. **Fix bad labels when they are a large share of the error you have left, and fix them sooner when the set is small.** "If the fraction of the dev set that is mislabeled impedes your ability to make these judgments, then it is worth spending time to fix the mislabeled dev set labels." (S001) "If you have only a hundred examples, these little labeling errors, if 10 of your hundred labels are wrong, that actually is 10% of your data set has a big impact." (S003) At 10% error, 0.6 points from bad labels is optional. At 2% error, the same 0.6 points is worth the time (S001). Apply the same cleaning to the test set (S001). Teams tolerate dirty labels early and change their minds as the system improves (S001). Newer (2020, S003): on a hundred industrial examples, clean the rules instead of averaging. Averaging is for a billion users. Sources: 2 (S001, S003). Confidence: medium.
16. **Give the team one number to rank models, hold the other goals to pass/fail bars, and set a business metric beside the ML metric.** "Having a single-number evaluation metric speeds up your ability to make a decision when you are selecting among a large number of classifiers." (S001) "Unfortunately, not every business problem can be reduced to optimizing test set accuracy!" (S002) The 2018 bars are things like accuracy subject to a running-time cap, or false negatives subject to one false positive a day (S001). If the metric prefers a system you would not ship, you can no longer trust it (S001). Newer (2022, S002): milestones have to include a business number such as revenue, not only accuracy. The single ML number stays. Sources: 2 (S001, S002). Confidence: medium.
17. **On a new problem, set a rough dev set and metric quickly, and replace them when they point the wrong way.** "It is better to come up with something imperfect and get going quickly, rather than overthink this." (S001) "If you aren’t able to determine reasonable milestones, it may be a sign that you need to learn more about the problem." (S002) He asks teams for an initial dev set, test set, and metric in under a week (S001). That week does not apply to mature applications (S001). When the sets or the metric stop pointing the right way, change them. He treats that as ordinary, not as a crisis (S001). Newer (2022, S002): a total inability to name milestones means you do not understand the problem yet, so learn more before you build. The two rules fit together. Ship a rough metric fast, and if you cannot name a milestone at all, stop and learn more. inferred from S001, S002. Sources: 2 (S001, S002). Confidence: medium.
18. **Make the dev set large enough to see the gain you care about, and no larger than the evaluation needs.** "The dev set should be large enough to detect differences between algorithms that you are trying out." (S001) "There is no need to have excessively large dev/test sets beyond what is needed to evaluate the performance of your algorithms." (S001) 100 examples cannot see a 0.1% change. 1,000 to 10,000 is a common dev set (S001). Older (2012, S004): a hold-out of about 30% is fine when data is abundant or cheap, and when data is scarce use 10-fold cross validation or leave-one-out. Newer (2018, S001): once data is plentiful, the old 70/30 split does not apply, and dev and test can be much less than 30% of the data. Sources: 2 (S001, S004). Confidence: medium.
19. **Treat a clean test score as the start of the job, and plan for a changing environment, the software around the model, and drift.** "There's a huge gulf between something that works in a Jupyter notebook on your laptop versus something that runs in a production deployment setting" (S003) "Even after the system has hit the target, further iteration may be necessary to address post-deployment drift." (S002) The model is maybe 5% of the code or less, and teams underestimate the rest (S003). You also cannot promise an accuracy date up front (S002). Redesign the workflow around the step you automated (S003). Sources: 2 (S002, S003). Confidence: medium.
20. **Prefer a pipeline of simpler stages when you do not have a lot of labeled pairs for an end-to-end model.** "When this type of data is not available, approach end-to-end learning with great caution." (S001) "Until more end-to-end data becomes available, I believe the non-end-to-end approach is significantly more promising for autonomous driving" (S001) Hand-built stages let a system learn with less data, and each stage should be a simple function you can label (S001). If every stage is near human level and the whole system is not, the pipeline is missing information (S001). S003 agrees on the practical shape: automate a step, then redesign the work around it, and judge driving by ordinary failures such as not braking. The 2022 phrase about building an end-to-end system quickly is a product slice, not a reversal of this architecture rule. inferred from S001, S002. Sources: 2 (S001, S003). Confidence: medium.
21. **Start from a business problem and a customer who would accept the result, and stop when there is no good AI answer.** "Identify a business problem (not an AI problem)." (S002) "So as you build a startup, you have to constantly ask the question, will the customer give a thumbs up on this?" (S003) Sometimes there is no good AI solution, and that is acceptable (S002). If you cannot set milestones, you probably do not understand the problem yet (S002). A common way startups fail is building something nobody wanted (S003). You do not get to vote on your own success. The customer does (S003). A paper result is far from a system you can ship (S003). Sources: 2 (S002, S003). Confidence: medium.
22. **Take the project only if it is good for people, including when the revenue case already works.** "It goes without saying that we should only work on projects that are responsible, ethical, and beneficial to people." (S002) "the business case was fine, the revenue case was fine, but I looked at it and just said, I don't want to do this." (S003) The second line is a company he cut because it would have made people watch many more videos in a narrow area (S003). He also refuses products whose point is addiction in order to sell ads (S003). S002 states the filter as a general rule, not as a private preference. Sources: 2 (S002, S003). Confidence: medium.
23. **As tools become turnkey, ship them with less math, and still learn the concepts that choose the next step.** "As machine learning techniques mature and become more reliable and turnkey, they require less debugging, and a shallower understanding of the math involved may be sufficient to make them work." (S002) "Beyond specific models, it’s even more important to understand the core concepts behind how and why machine learning works" (S002) Newer (2022, S002): depth of math follows the maturity of the tool, and you learn what the decision in front of you requires. Older (2012, S005): he still wants the theory, because it should hone intuition and turn into rules of thumb. The concepts he names in 2022 are bias and variance, cost functions, regularization, optimization algorithms, and error analysis, not the algebra of one model. Sources: 2 (S002, S005). Confidence: medium.
24. **Bet on scale in products, and do not bet the product on unsupervised learning or on reinforcement learning being what works today.** "The thing we really got wrong was the importance of, the early importance of unsupervised learning." (S003) "It's not what's working today." (S003) The second quote is reinforcement learning, February 2020. He still loves the field, his thesis was in it, and he still teaches it, but he has shrunk how much he teaches because it is not what is deployed. He adds that the world changes fast and this could be different in a couple of years (S003). Older (2012, S009): use a reward when you cannot hand the learner the correct action, only a signal of doing well or poorly. That course framing is real. The 2020 deployment caution is newer, and he holds it lightly. The early unsupervised bet is the thing he says Google Brain got wrong. A 2005 argument from Hinton had convinced him unsupervised learning would matter, and he now calls that early (S003). The part he says they got right is scale (S003). He also treats far-off AGI stories as a distraction from harder problems already in front of teams, such as a car that fails to brake (S003). Sources: 2 (S003, S009). Confidence: medium.
25. **Do not use the test set to choose anything, including a rollback, and do not wait on a significance test for interim progress.** "But do not use the test set to make any decisions regarding the algorithm, including whether to roll back to the previous week’s system." (S001) "I usually do not find statistical significance tests useful for measuring interim progress." (S001) A regular test-set read is allowed when you only want to track the team (S001). Decisions stay on the dev set. Sources: 1 (S001). Confidence: low.
26. **Attribute the error to search, to the score, or to one pipeline stage before you assign the work.** "By attributing errors to specific parts of the pipeline, you can decide how to prioritize your work." (S001) "unless you know which of these is the underlying cause of the error, your efforts could be wasted." (S001) If 95% of errors are the scoring function, search work can remove at most about 5%. If 90 of 100 errors sit in one stage, that stage is the job (S001). The same split decides whether a bad reinforcement learner needs a new reward or a new algorithm (S001). Sources: 1 (S001). Confidence: low.
27. **Do not pool datasets that would give the same input two different labels.** "If you only care about predicting New York City housing prices, putting the two datasets together will hurt your performance." (S001) "If you can afford to build a neural network with a large enough number of hidden units/layers, you can safely add the 20,000 images to your training set." (S001) Pool when one input-to-label mapping fits both sources and the model is large and flexible. Leave out data that teaches nothing, to save compute (S001). If the extra source is much larger or very different, down-weight it and tune the weight on the dev set, instead of growing a giant net to swallow it (S001). Older practice, described in the same 2018 book: mixing sources was risky with earlier algorithms, and engineers warned against it. Newer, in that book: the risk has greatly diminished for large flexible networks. The label-consistency test is unchanged. Sources: 1 (S001). Confidence: low.
28. **Treat error analysis as a guide, and trust it most on tasks humans can already do.** "Error analysis does not produce a rigid mathematical formula that tells you what the highest priority task should be." (S001) "Many error analysis processes work best when we are trying to automate something humans can do and can thus benchmark against human-level performance." (S001) Choose by the share of errors, the progress you expect, and the effort. There is no formula (S001). You will overfit the slice you stare at, which is why a large dev set needs an Eyeball slice and a Blackbox slice (S001). The 2018 book aims this advice at people building applications, not at people whose goal is an academic paper (S001). If humans cannot do the task, much of the ceiling, the human proxy, and the hand count does not apply (S001). Sources: 1 (S001). Confidence: low.
